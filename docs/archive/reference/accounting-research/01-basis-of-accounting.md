# 01 — Basis of accounting

**Questions (from the outline):** Cash vs mercantile (accrual) vs a mixed basis. What each entity type uses in practice: household, sole trader, partnership, joint family, trust and committee. What a book must state about its basis. The fundamental assumptions (going concern, consistency, accrual) and the considerations in choosing a policy (prudence, substance over form, materiality).
**Settles (tracing only):** F-03, GAPS F2

How the sources were read. NCERT and ICAI BoS PDFs were downloaded and their full text extracted and read (`fetched`). AS 1 was first read through the ICAI "Ind AS access" web pages as an extract; every AS 1 citation was then checked against the ICAI PDF (owner-supplied copy `89095asb-aps2918-as1.pdf`, 20 Oct 2026). The ICAI Framework citations (¶22–24, 30, 35, 37) were checked word for word against the full ICAI PDF (ASB, issued July 2000; owner-supplied copy `89089asb-aps2918-framework.pdf`, checked 20 Oct 2026).

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Cash basis
Under the cash basis an entry is made only when cash is received or paid, not when the receipt or payment falls due. NCERT's own example: rent for December paid in January is recorded in January; a credit sale made in January and paid in April is recorded in April. Profit is then the difference between receipts and disbursements of money for the period. NCERT calls it simple but "inappropriate for most organisations" and "incompatible with the matching principle". ICAI BoS states the same as a formula: cash received less cash paid in the ordinary course of business equals profit.
**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Financial Accounting I, ch. 2 §2.4 "Basis of Accounting" (printed pp. 36–37) — fetched. ICAI BoS, Foundation Paper 1, ch. 1 unit 2, "Alternative as per Cash basis" under accrual concept (d) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: December rent ₹8,000 paid on 5 January: `5 Jan: Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹8,000` (nothing is entered in December). Plain words: "5 January: paid ₹8,000 rent (for December) in cash."

### 1.2 Accrual (mercantile) basis
Under the accrual basis revenues and costs are recognised when they are earned or incurred, not when money is received or paid, and are recorded in the period to which they relate. A distinction is made between receipt of cash and the right to receive it, and between payment of cash and the obligation to pay it. AS 1 states it as the third fundamental assumption: "Revenues and costs are accrued, that is, recognised as they are earned or incurred." The Framework (¶22) says the effects of transactions "are recognised when they occur". ICAI BoS equates accrual with "mercantile basis".
**Source:** AS 1 ¶10(c) — fetched (ICAI PDF). Framework ¶22 — fetched (ICAI PDF). NCERT ch. 2 §2.4 — fetched. ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, ch. 1 unit 2, accrual concept (d) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: March credit sale to Kapoor Traders, money received in April: `31 Mar: Dr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹10,000`; `10 Apr: Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹10,000`. Plain words: "31 March: sold goods ₹10,000 to Kapoor Traders, to be paid later. 10 April: Kapoor Traders paid ₹10,000 in cash."

### 1.3 What accrual creates: receivable, payable, advance, prepaid
ICAI BoS lists the four timing cases between cash and recognition: cash received before the revenue is booked creates a liability; cash received after creates a receivable; cash paid before the expense is booked creates an advance (asset); cash paid after creates a payable or outstanding liability. The journal proper carries the year-end adjusting entries for outstanding and prepaid items, and for income received in advance or earned but not received.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, ch. 1 unit 2, table after "Alternative as per Cash basis" — fetched; ch. 2 unit 4 (Subsidiary Books), "Adjusting entries" under journal proper — fetched. NCERT ch. 4 §4.6 Journal Proper item 2 "Adjustment Entries" (Rent outstanding, Prepaid insurance, Commission received in advance) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: December rent ₹8,000 unpaid at 31 December: `31 Dec: Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Outstanding Rent A/c ₹8,000`. Insurance ₹12,000 paid on 1 October for twelve months, books closed 31 December, ₹3,000 has been used: `31 Dec: Dr Prepaid Insurance A/c ₹9,000 · Cr Insurance A/c ₹9,000` (illustration built from the rule). Plain words: "31 December: December rent ₹8,000 is owed, not yet paid." / "31 December: of the ₹12,000 insurance paid, ₹9,000 is for next year, so it is carried forward."

### 1.4 Mixed basis
The sources read name two bases only, cash and accrual (NCERT §2.4: "two broad approaches"). None of AS 1, the Framework, NCERT or ICAI BoS defines or permits a named "mixed" or "hybrid" basis. What the sources do describe is incomplete-records practice: a small business keeps a cash book and personal accounts, records some items on one side only, and finds its result by comparing capital at two dates (see 1.9 and topic 04). That is a practice, not a basis the sources endorse. See section 3.
**Source:** NCERT ch. 2 §2.4 — fetched. ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 2 (Module 2), ch. 9 "Accounts from Incomplete Records" §1 and §2 — fetched. AS 1 ¶10 — fetched (ICAI PDF).
**Example.** Professional (a shop keeping only personal accounts and cash): credit sale to Sunita, ₹300: `Dr Sunita A/c ₹300` (credit side not recorded anywhere). Plain words: "Sunita owes ₹300 for bread and milk."

### 1.5 The fundamental assumptions: going concern, consistency, accrual
AS 1 ¶9–10: certain fundamental assumptions underlie financial statements, namely going concern ("continuing in operation for the foreseeable future"), consistency ("accounting policies are consistent from one period to another"), and accrual. The Framework gives the same three at ¶22 (accrual), ¶23 (going concern: no intention or need to liquidate or curtail materially the scale of operations), ¶24 (consistency).
NCERT's going-concern example: a computer bought for ₹50,000 with a five-year life is charged ₹10,000 a year, because the business is assumed to continue; without the continuity assumption the whole ₹50,000 would be charged in the year of purchase. NCERT's consistency note: consistency does not forbid a change of policy; a necessary change is disclosed with its probable effect. ICAI BoS adds that if going concern does not hold, statements may have to be prepared on a different basis, and the basis used must be disclosed.
**Source:** AS 1 ¶9–10 — fetched (ICAI PDF). Framework ¶22–24 — fetched (ICAI PDF). NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.3 (p. 28–29) and §2.2.10 (p. 33–34) — fetched. ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, ch. 1 unit 2, going concern concept (f) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Computer A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹50,000` on purchase; each year `Dr Depreciation A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Computer A/c ₹10,000`, the same method every year. Plain words: "Bought a computer ₹50,000 cash. Each year, ₹10,000 of it is used up and charged to that year."

### 1.6 What the book must state about its basis and policies
AS 1: if the three fundamental assumptions are followed, specific disclosure is not required; if one is not followed, the fact must be disclosed (¶27). All significant accounting policies should be disclosed in the financial statements and normally in one place (¶18–¶20 explain it; ¶24–¶25 state it as a main principle). NCERT/BoS add that a change of policy is disclosed with its effect. Reading these together: a statement that is silent on its basis is read as going concern, consistent and accrual; a cash-basis book therefore departs from the assumed basis and has to say so.
**Source:** AS 1 ¶27, ¶24 (with ¶18) — fetched (ICAI PDF). NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.10 — fetched. ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2 (f) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: the entry `Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹8,000` on 5 January is a cash-basis entry; the statement head reads "Basis: cash — entries made when money is received or paid". Plain words: "Paid ₹8,000 rent in cash." Header note: "This book records money when it moves."

### 1.7 Prudence
AS 1 ¶17(a): in view of uncertainty about future events, profits are not anticipated but recognised only when realised; known liabilities and losses are provided for even if the amount is uncertain. Framework ¶37: assets or income are not overstated and liabilities or expenses not understated. NCERT §2.2.11 "Conservatism (Prudence)": record no profit until realised, provide for all losses even remote ones; examples are closing stock at cost or market value whichever is lower and a provision for doubtful debts. NCERT warns that deliberate understatement creates "secret reserves" and should be discouraged.
**Source:** AS 1 ¶17(a) — fetched (ICAI PDF). Framework ¶37 — fetched (ICAI PDF). NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.11 (p. 34) — fetched. ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2, conservatism concept — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: Ramesh, a debtor of ₹5,000, is declared insolvent: `Dr Bad Debts A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Ramesh A/c ₹5,000`. If goods are worth ₹3,000 more than cost, no entry is made until they are sold. Plain words: "Ramesh cannot pay the ₹5,000 he owes; written off as a loss." / "Goods are worth more than they cost; nothing is written until sold."

### 1.8 Substance over form
AS 1 ¶17(b): transactions are accounted for and presented according to their substance and not merely their legal form. Framework ¶35: "substance and economic reality and not merely their legal form". The sources read give no rupee illustration of their own; one case the BoS timing table does settle is that money received before the sale is booked is a liability, whatever the receipt is called.
**Source:** AS 1 ¶17(b) — fetched (ICAI PDF). Framework ¶35 — fetched (ICAI PDF). ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2 timing table — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: ₹10,000 received from Kapoor Traders before goods are supplied: `Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Kapoor Traders (Advance) A/c ₹10,000`. Plain words: "Kapoor Traders paid ₹10,000 in advance for goods not yet supplied; we owe them the goods."

### 1.9 Materiality
AS 1 ¶17(c): items are disclosed if knowledge of them might influence the decisions of a user. Framework ¶30: information is material if omitting or misstating it could influence economic decisions. NCERT §2.2.12: where the amount is very small, strict adherence is not required; stationery bought is treated as the expense of the period "whether consumed or not". ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2 (l): materiality "depends not only upon the amount of the item but also upon the size of the business, nature and level of information", and small items such as books or calculators are written off in full in the year of purchase.
**Source:** AS 1 ¶17(c) — fetched (ICAI PDF). Framework ¶30 — fetched (ICAI PDF). NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.12 (p. 34–35) — fetched. ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2 (l) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Stationery A/c ₹300 · Cr Cash A/c ₹300` (all of it charged to the year even if part is unused). Plain words: "Bought pens and paper ₹300 cash; all treated as this year's expense."

### 1.10 The accounting period
NCERT §2.2.4: financial statements are prepared at regular intervals, normally one year; an interim period may be needed, for example when a partner retires. ICAI BoS: one year is usual, 1 April to 31 March in India, but 6, 9 or 15 months are possible; accrual only has meaning against a finite period.
**Source:** NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.4 — fetched. ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2, periodicity concept (c) — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: year-end accrual at 31 March: `31 Mar: Dr Wages A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Outstanding Wages A/c ₹6,000`; paid on 5 April: `5 Apr: Dr Outstanding Wages A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹6,000`. Plain words: "31 March: ₹6,000 wages owed for March. 5 April: paid it."

### 1.11 What each entity type uses in practice
- **Sole trader.** NCERT and ICAI BoS teach accrual for the sole proprietor's final accounts (trading and profit and loss account, balance sheet, with adjustment entries). Small sole traders and small partnerships often keep incomplete records: cash book and personal accounts, results found from capital comparison (ICAI BoS Module 2 ch. 9 §1–3).
- **Partnership.** NCERT Class 12 Part I ch. 1 §1.4 describes partners' dealings with the firm as recorded through capital accounts in the firm's books; it names no basis. ICAI BoS ch. 9 says single entry is found "in partnership firms to some extent". No source read names a basis for partnerships.
- **Trust and committee (not-for-profit).** The books produce a Receipts and Payments Account, an Income and Expenditure Account and a Balance Sheet. The Receipts and Payments Account is "a summary of the cash book" and "is not based on the accrual system": it records all receipts and payments, capital or revenue, of any period. The Income and Expenditure Account "is prepared by following accrual principle" with adjustments for outstanding and advance items.
- **Household and joint family.** None of the sources read says what basis a household or a joint family uses. See section 3.
- **Traditional merchants (bahi-khata).** A university IKS course summary of B.M. Lall Nigam states that "most Indian traders follow cash basis of accounting" and record credit sales by debiting personal accounts directly. A management-college course PDF describes the Gujarati "Deshi Nama" books as also holding nominal accounts and year-end adjustments (outstanding and prepaid expenses, closing stock). Both are secondary (see topic 03).
**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 2 ch. 8 §2, §3 and chapter summary; ch. 9 §1–3 and summary — fetched. NCERT Class 11 Part II ch. 8, §8.6–8.7 — fetched. NCERT Class 12 Part I ch. 1 §1.4 — fetched. Shivaji University CDOE, B.Com II sem. III IKS, "Evolution of Accounting in India", unit 1, §a — fetched (course summary of Nigam 1986, not the paper). CESS management course PDF "Journey of Indian Accounting Practices", part 2 — fetched (secondary).
**Example.** Professional (trust, subscription ₹1,200 received for the year, of which ₹300 is for next year): Receipts and Payments shows `Dr Cash A/c ₹1,200 · Cr Subscriptions A/c ₹1,200` in full; Income and Expenditure shows ₹900 as income and ₹300 as advance (illustration built from the rule). Plain words: "Members paid ₹1,200 subscriptions. The cash statement shows ₹1,200; the surplus statement counts only ₹900 for this year."

## 2. Where the sources disagree, or are silent
- **AS 1 disclosure paragraph number — settled by the PDF (20 Oct 2026).** Both fetches were right: AS 1 says it twice, in the explanation (¶18) and in the bold-italic *Main Principles* (¶24). Cite ¶24 with ¶18.
- **Mixed basis.** AS 1, the Framework, NCERT and ICAI BoS are silent on a mixed or hybrid basis. ICAI BoS ch. 9 describes partial-record practice but calls single entry "a system of incomplete or improper records", not a basis.
- **Cash basis of traders.** The Shivaji IKS summary (of Nigam) says most traders are on cash basis; the CESS course PDF describes accrual-type year-end adjustments in the Deshi Nama books. Both are course material, neither read in the original.
- **Household and joint family:** no source read addresses basis.
- **Framework version.** The 2000 ICAI Framework (assumptions at ¶22–24) applies with the existing AS; the Ind AS Framework lists only accrual and going concern. The extract read was the 2000 Framework.
- **Substance over form:** no rupee illustration in NCERT or BoS Foundation; the example in 1.8 rests on the BoS timing table, not on a text that names substance over form.

## 4. Answers to the GAPS items this topic settles
- **F2 (prepaid / outstanding at year end).** Practice on the accrual basis: a payment made ahead of the period is carried as an asset (prepaid), an amount owed and unpaid is carried as a liability (outstanding), each by a year-end journal entry (ICAI BoS ch. 2 unit 4, adjusting entries; NCERT ch. 4 §4.6 item 2). On the cash basis there is no such entry (NCERT §2.4). Whether a wizard drives it is not an accounting question and no source addresses it. The basis a book is on decides whether F2 applies at all; see section 3.
- **F-03.** Tracing only; the audit text was not read. This note answers the topic's question: the sources name cash and accrual only, and require the basis to be stated when it departs from the assumed accrual basis (AS 1 ¶27).
