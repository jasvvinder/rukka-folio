# 09 — Assets and deposits

**Questions (from the outline):** Capital vs revenue expenditure. Assets at cost (AS 10). Depreciation: whether a small or cash-basis book may omit it, and what it must then say. Sale or disposal. Construction in progress vs expense. Fixed and recurring deposits and investments. Security deposits held and paid.
**Settles (tracing only):** GAPS A1, A3, E2; F-04; D-opt-1

How sources were read. "NCERT" = NCERT Accountancy, Class 11, *Financial Accounting* Part I and Part II, reprint 2026-27; the PDFs were downloaded and their text read (pages are the printed page numbers). ICAI pronouncements (AS 5, 9, 10, 13) were read through the ICAI Accounting Standards Board pages at indasaccess.icai.org; the reader returned excerpts, so their paragraph numbers are as returned and a bookkeeper should confirm them against the full PDF. The Framework citations (¶22, 30, 49, 81, 93, 95, 96) were checked word for word against the full ICAI PDF (ASB, issued July 2000; owner-supplied copy `89089asb-aps2918-framework.pdf`, checked 20 Oct 2026). Ledger-rule examples use invented names.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Capital expenditure vs revenue expenditure
Money spent for a benefit that lasts more than one accounting year is capital expenditure and is carried as an asset; money spent for a benefit used up within the year is revenue expenditure and is an expense of the year. NCERT lists the usual tests: capital expenditure acquires or adds to a fixed asset, is non-recurring, and benefits more than one year; revenue expenditure keeps the earning capacity going, recurs, and benefits one year (Part II ch 8, §8.2.1, pp. 279–280). Part I ch 1 §1.5.9 says the same in one line. The ICAI Framework gives the same result from the definition of an asset: a resource controlled as a result of past events from which future economic benefits are expected (Framework ¶49(a)); expenses are recognised when future benefits decrease (¶93), spread by systematic allocation when benefit arises over several periods (¶95), and at once when the expenditure produces no future benefit (¶96). Putting a capital item in an expense head overstates expense and understates assets, and the reverse overstates profit (NCERT Part II ch 8, §8.2.3, p. 281, worked example of ₹20,000 repairs wrongly capitalised).
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch 8 §8.2.1–8.2.3 pp. 279–281 — fetched (ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/keac201.pdf); NCERT Part I ch 1 §1.5.9 — fetched (keac101.pdf); ICAI Framework ¶49, 93, 95, 96 — fetched (ICAI PDF) https://indasaccess.icai.org/Volume-III/AS/asb.html?a=101
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Furniture A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹12,000` (a cupboard that will serve for years); `Dr Salaries A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹12,000` (a month's wages, used up). Plain words: "Bought a steel cupboard for the shop, paid ₹12,000 cash — it stays with us as a thing owned." / "Paid ₹12,000 wages for the month in cash — gone."

### 1.2 A very small purchase is expensed, however long it lasts
Where the amount is very small, strict application of the principle is not required: NCERT's example is stationery, which is never shown as an asset, and whatever is bought in the year is treated as that year's expense whether or not it is consumed. The textbook gives no rupee threshold. **Source:** NCERT Part I ch 2 §2.2.12 (Materiality), pp. 34–35 — fetched; the Framework ¶30 says information is material if its omission or misstatement could influence users — fetched (ICAI PDF).
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Stationery A/c ₹1,500 · Cr Cash A/c ₹1,500`. Plain words: "Bought pens, files and a stapler for ₹1,500 cash — written off as stationery for the year."

### 1.3 An asset is carried at its cost, and cost includes what it takes to put it to use
Cost is the price paid together with the costs necessary to bring the asset to working condition: freight and transport, transit insurance, installation, registration, commission on purchase, and, for a second-hand asset, initial repairs. The photocopier example is ₹50,000 + ₹5,000 transport and installation = ₹55,000 cost. AS 10 says the same: cost includes costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary (¶17(b), ¶18: site preparation, initial delivery and handling, installation and assembly, testing, professional fees) and excludes costs of opening a new facility, introducing a new product, general overheads (¶20), initial operating losses and relocation costs (¶21).
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch 7 §7.5.1, pp. 232–233 — fetched (keac107.pdf); ICAI AS 10 (revised 2016) ¶17(b), 18, 20, 21 — fetched (extract) https://indasaccess.icai.org/Volume-III/AS/asb.html?a=111
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Machine A/c ₹55,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹55,000` (₹50,000 price + ₹5,000 carriage and fitting, paid together). Plain words: "Bought a flour mill machine, ₹50,000, and paid ₹5,000 for bringing and fitting it — all ₹55,000 is the cost of the machine."

### 1.4 Repairs are expense; additions and improvements are added to cost
The costs of day-to-day servicing (typically repairs and maintenance) are recognised in the profit and loss as incurred, not capitalised (AS 10 ¶12). Replacement of a part, or an addition or extension that becomes part of the asset, is capitalised when incurred if the recognition criteria are met and the replaced part is derecognised (AS 10 ¶13); NCERT states that such addition is "in addition to usual repair and maintenance expenses" and is capitalised and depreciated over the asset's life (Part I ch 7 §7.10, p. 259). Classifying an addition as a repair is an error of principle (NCERT Part I ch 6 §6.4.4, p. 190).
**Source:** AS 10 ¶12, 13 — fetched (extract); NCERT Part I ch 7 §7.10 p. 259 and ch 6 §6.4.4 p. 190 — fetched. Corroborated by ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 1 Unit 3 §3.2(c)–(d), p. 1.63 (ordinary repairs are revenue; expenditure on improvement is added to the asset where expected future benefits increase) — fetched (ICAI BoS PDF).
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Repairs A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹2,000` (fixing a leaking tap); `Dr Shop Building A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹30,000` (new steel shutters fitted). Plain words: "Paid ₹2,000 plumber for a tap — repairs." / "Paid ₹30,000 for new shutters on the shop — added to the shop's cost."

### 1.5 Depreciation is a charge for the part of cost used up in the year
Depreciation is the part of the cost of a depreciable asset that has expired through use, passage of time or obsolescence; it is charged against the year's income and is a non-cash expense (NCERT Part I ch 7 §7.1, §7.1.2 point 5, pp. 226–229). AS 10 requires the depreciable amount (cost less residual value) to be allocated on a systematic basis over the useful life (¶52), the charge to be recognised in the profit and loss (¶50), depreciation to begin when the asset is available for use (¶57), residual value and useful life to be reviewed at least each year-end (¶53), the method to reflect the pattern of benefit (¶62), be reviewed yearly (¶63) and be applied consistently (¶64). Straight line: (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life; written-down value is the other usual method (NCERT §7.6.1, §7.6.2, pp. 234–238). Assets with no limited useful life are outside the definition of "depreciable" (NCERT Part I ch 7 Box 1, p. 228 — assets expected to be used for more than a year, with a limited useful life, held for use not sale), so land, a plot and the like are not depreciated by that definition; **gold is not covered by any source I read** (see §3). The year-end entry: `Dr Depreciation A/c · Cr Asset A/c` (asset shown at cost less depreciation), or the credit goes to a Provision for Depreciation A/c and the asset stays at cost (NCERT §7.8, pp. 240–241; Part II ch 9 §9.7, pp. 327–329).
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch 7 §7.1–7.8; Part II ch 9 §9.7 — fetched; AS 10 ¶50, 52, 53, 57, 62–64 — fetched (extract).
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Depreciation A/c ₹1,500 · Cr Furniture A/c ₹1,500` (furniture ₹15,000 at 10% a year, NCERT's own figures, Part II §9.7). Plain words: "At year end, wrote off ₹1,500 of the ₹15,000 furniture as wear for the year — furniture now stands at ₹13,500; profit is ₹1,500 lower."

### 1.6 Whether a small or cash-basis book may omit depreciation
No source I could read gives a small or cash-basis book permission to omit it. What the sources do say: (a) NCERT says that if depreciation is not provided "the assets will be over valued and the balance sheet will not depict the correct financial position... this is not permitted either by established accounting practices or by specific provisions of law" (§7.4.3, p. 232); (b) AS 10's only small-entity relief is that a micro and small-sized enterprise "may not comply with paragraph 87", the paragraph on *encouraged* disclosures, not on recognition or depreciation (¶87 proviso); (c) the Framework says failure to recognise an item "is not rectified by disclosure of the accounting policies used" (¶81), and states the accrual assumption (¶22); (d) AS 1 (as shown on the ICAI page) requires that if a fundamental accounting assumption such as accrual is not followed the fact be disclosed; (e) an ICAI Expert Advisory Committee opinion, seen only in a search extract, says that where depreciation is not provided the auditor should qualify the report or give a negative opinion depending on materiality (about a company; primary URL https://eacopinion.icai.org/html/9/4.htm — not read in full). The sources therefore say neither "may omit" nor give a form of words for a book that does. A book that leaves it out is carrying its assets at cost with no write-down; what it should print is not settled (§3, row 1).
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch 7 §7.4.3 p. 232 — fetched; AS 10 ¶87 proviso — fetched (extract); Framework ¶22, ¶81 — fetched (ICAI PDF); AS 1 ¶27 on disclosure of departures — fetched (ICAI PDF); EAC opinion — search https://eacopinion.icai.org/html/9/4.htm
**Example.** (What the omission means in numbers.) Professional: with depreciation `Dr Depreciation A/c ₹9,000 · Cr Computer A/c ₹9,000`; without it, no entry, so the computer stays at ₹45,000 cost and the year's result is ₹9,000 higher. Plain words: "Computer bought for ₹45,000, three-year life. If we do not write off ₹9,000 this year, the computer still shows ₹45,000 and the profit is ₹9,000 more than the wear it caused."

### 1.7 Land and other assets of unlimited life are carried at cost
By the depreciable-asset definition in §1.5, an asset without a limited useful life is carried at cost and not written down; its cost still includes the costs of acquisition (§1.3). No source I read says this of land by name; the inference rests on NCERT Box 1 (p. 228). **Source:** NCERT Part I ch 7 Box 1 p. 228 — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Plot A/c ₹12,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹12,00,000`; stamp duty and registry ₹60,000: `Dr Plot A/c ₹60,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹60,000`. Plain words: "Bought a plot for ₹12,00,000; paid ₹60,000 stamp duty and registry — the plot now stands at ₹12,60,000."

### 1.8 Sale or disposal: profit or loss is proceeds less carrying amount
On disposal the carrying amount is removed from the books; the gain or loss is the difference between net disposal proceeds and the carrying amount and is taken to the profit and loss, and gains on disposal are not classified as revenue (AS 10 ¶74, ¶75, ¶79). NCERT: the sale price is credited to the asset account (or to an asset-disposal account) and the balance is the profit or loss; if a provision for depreciation is kept, it is first transferred to the asset account (Part I ch 7 §7.9, §7.9.1, pp. 249–251). Where the asset is carried at cost with no depreciation, the carrying amount is cost.
**Source:** AS 10 ¶74, 75, 79 — fetched (extract); NCERT Part I ch 7 §7.9 pp. 249–251 — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: sold at a profit: `Dr Bank A/c ₹15,50,000 · Cr Plot A/c ₹12,00,000 · Cr Profit on Sale of Plot A/c ₹3,50,000`. Sold at a loss: `Dr Bank A/c ₹40,000 · Dr Loss on Sale of Scooter A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Scooter A/c ₹50,000`. Plain words: "Sold the plot for ₹15,50,000; it stood in our books at ₹12,00,000 — ₹3,50,000 gain." / "Sold the scooter for ₹40,000; it stood at ₹50,000 — ₹10,000 loss."

### 1.9 Purchase of an asset on credit goes through the journal, not the purchases book
A credit purchase of an item other than goods (machinery, furniture) is a journal proper entry; recording it in the purchases book is an error of principle (NCERT Part I ch 4 §4.6 item 5(ii), p. 140; ch 6 §6.4.4 p. 190). **Source:** both fetched.
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Furniture A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Rao Furnishings A/c ₹8,000`. Plain words: "Took a table and chairs from Rao Furnishings for ₹8,000, to pay later — owed to Rao, and the furniture is ours."

### 1.10 Construction in progress: cost of building is an asset; general overheads and start-up losses are not
Expenditure on constructing an asset (bought or self-built) is recognised as an asset if it is probable the asset will bring future benefits and its cost can be measured reliably (AS 10 ¶7). A self-constructed asset's cost is determined on the same principles as a bought one, with abnormal wastage excluded (¶23); directly attributable costs (¶17(b), ¶18) are included, general overheads and initial operating losses are not (¶20, ¶21). A project may be treated as one unit for capitalising construction-period expenditure (¶9) and the standard requires disclosure of expenditure recognised in an asset's carrying amount during construction (¶82(b)). NCERT Class 11 does not discuss construction in progress. What the books print for an asset not yet in use (heading, depreciation start) is: depreciation begins when the asset is available for use (¶57); no heading is prescribed in the extract I read.
**Source:** AS 10 ¶7, 9, 17(b), 18, 20, 21, 23, 57, 82(b) — fetched (extract) https://indasaccess.icai.org/Volume-III/AS/asb.html?a=111
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Hall Under Construction A/c ₹2,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹2,00,000` (cement, steel, mason's wages for the new hall). Plain words: "Paid ₹2,00,000 for materials and labour on the new hall — adds to the hall's cost; it is not an expense of the year." Contrast: `Dr Office Expenses A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹6,000` (the committee's general running costs the same month).

### 1.11 A fixed or recurring deposit is money placed with the bank, carried at the amount placed; interest is income
AS 13 defines investments as assets held for earning income by way of dividends, interest and rentals, or capital appreciation (¶3.1), carries long-term investments at cost (¶32) and takes the difference between carrying amount and net disposal proceeds to profit and loss on disposal (¶34). The page contains no statement about deposits with banks, so whether an FD or RD is within AS 13 is not stated; the rules below are applied to deposits by analogy and that is flagged in §3. Interest is income: NCERT classes interest on investment as a revenue receipt (Part II ch 8 §8.2.2, p. 281); AS 9 says interest accrues on a time basis determined by the amount outstanding and the rate (¶8.2, ¶13(i)). So placing the deposit is not an expense; the interest credited is.
**Source:** AS 13 ¶3.1, 32, 34 — fetched (extract) https://indasaccess.icai.org/Volume-III/AS/asb.html?a=114; AS 9 ¶8.2, 13(i) — fetched (extract) https://indasaccess.icai.org/Volume-III/AS/asb.html?a=110; NCERT Part II ch 8 §8.2.2 p. 281 — fetched.
**Example.** Professional: placed `Dr Fixed Deposit A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000`; bank credits interest `Dr Bank A/c ₹4,000 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹4,000`; matures `Dr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Fixed Deposit A/c ₹1,00,000`. Plain words: "Put ₹1,00,000 in a fixed deposit — still ours, only kept aside." / "Bank added ₹4,000 interest — income." / "Deposit paid back ₹1,00,000."
(A recurring deposit instalment is the same shape for each instalment: `Dr Recurring Deposit A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹2,000`; no source I read describes RD accounting — applied by analogy, §3.)

### 1.12 A security deposit paid is an asset; one held is a liability
A deposit given to a landlord or supplier is a resource from which a future benefit (return or adjustment) is expected, so it meets the Framework's definition of an asset (¶49(a)); a deposit taken from a tenant is a present obligation to return it, a liability (¶49(b)). The textbooks I read do not discuss security deposits; this rule rests on the Framework definitions alone.
**Source:** ICAI Framework ¶49(a), (b) — fetched (ICAI PDF) https://indasaccess.icai.org/Volume-III/AS/asb.html?a=101
**Example.** Professional: paid `Dr Deposit Paid – Kapoor Properties A/c ₹60,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹60,000`; held `Dr Bank A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Deposit Held – Sharma (tenant) A/c ₹30,000`. Plain words: "Paid ₹60,000 deposit to Kapoor Properties for the shop — we will get it back." / "Took ₹30,000 deposit from tenant Sharma — we owe it back."

### 1.13 The BoS on depreciation: not charging it misstates profit and assets; land is not depreciated; very small items are written off at once
The BoS says that if an enterprise does not account for depreciation it "will not be considering loss in value" of its property, plant and equipment and "will not result in true profit or loss for the period", and the assets would be shown above their true value; depreciation is "an essential element of business expenditure". Property, plant and equipment are tangible items held for use, rental or administration that are expected to be used for more than twelve months. Depreciation begins when the asset is available for use, so an asset need not have been used to be depreciated. The amount follows cost, estimated useful life and residual value: a machine of ₹1,10,000 with a residual value of ₹10,000 and a life of 5 years is depreciated by ₹20,000 a year. Land is not depreciated (True/False key). Under the materiality concept "depreciation on small items like books, calculators etc. is taken as 100% in the year of purchase", because the amount is too small to show in the balance sheet; what is material depends on "the size of the business" as well as the amount. Small, low-value components that need frequent replacement may be charged to expense as incurred. The BoS names no rupee limit and gives no exemption for a small or cash-basis book.
**Source:** ICAI BoS, Foundation Paper 1 Accounting (Aug 2026 ed.), Ch. 5 *Depreciation and Amortisation*, §1.1, p. 5.2–5.4; §1.3, p. 5.5; §2, p. 5.6; True/False 14 and its answer, p. 5.41; Ch. 1 Unit 2, materiality, p. 1.48 — fetched (ICAI BoS PDF) https://resource.cdn.icai.org/74614bos60479-fnd-cp5.pdf; https://resource.cdn.icai.org/74600bos60479-fnd-cp1-u2.pdf. Study material, not a pronouncement.
**Example.** Professional: `Dr Depreciation A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Machinery A/c ₹20,000` (the BoS's own figures: ₹1,10,000 less ₹10,000 over 5 years); a calculator bought for ₹500: `Dr Office Expenses A/c ₹500 · Cr Cash A/c ₹500` (all charged in the year of purchase). Plain words: "The machine will serve five years; each year I write off ₹20,000 of its cost." / "Bought a ₹500 calculator; too small to carry as an asset, so it is all this year's cost."

### 1.14 The BoS on deposits: a fixed deposit's maturity is a capital receipt, its interest is revenue, and accrued interest is an asset
When a fixed deposit matures, the amount of the deposit is "the recovery of the deposit amount, and is a capital receipt"; the interest received with it is revenue. Interest on fixed deposits is shown on its own line in the profit and loss account. Interest earned but not yet received at the year end is added to the year's income and shown as an asset (*Accrued Interest*), by `Interest Accruing A/c Dr · To Interest Income A/c`. A student-deposit held by a school or college "remain[s] with the institution till the student finally leaves" and appears among current liabilities; the BoS says nothing of a security deposit *paid* by the book, or of a recurring deposit.
**Source:** ICAI BoS, Foundation Paper 1 Accounting (Aug 2026 ed.), Ch. 1 Unit 3 §3.4 Illustration 4(d), p. 1.67; Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.4, p. 7.19 and adjustment (2), p. 7.24; Ch. 8 §5.2.3, p. 8.31 and Illustration 12, pp. 8.33, 8.36 — fetched (ICAI BoS PDF) https://resource.cdn.icai.org/74601bos60479-fnd-cp1-u3.pdf; https://resource.cdn.icai.org/74616bos60479-fnd-cp7-u1.pdf; https://resource.cdn.icai.org/74619bos60479-fnd-cp8.pdf. Study material, not a pronouncement.
**Example.** Professional: a fixed deposit of ₹5,00,000 matures with ₹40,000 interest: `Dr Bank A/c ₹5,40,000 · Cr Fixed Deposit A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹40,000`; at the year end with ₹10,000 of interest not yet received: `Dr Accrued Interest A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹10,000`. Plain words: "My fixed deposit matured: ₹5,00,000 is my own money coming back, ₹40,000 is interest I earned." / "The bank owes me ₹10,000 interest up to 31 March; counted as this year's income."

## 2. Where the sources disagree, or are silent
- **NCERT cites the withdrawn AS 6 for depreciation** (Part I ch 7 §7.1.1 and Box 1). Search results from ICAI show AS 6 withdrawn for non-corporate entities from periods beginning 1 April 2017, depreciation passing to AS 10 (revised 2016) (search: https://icai.org/post/amendment-to-as-2-4-6-10-13-14-21-and-29-issued-by-the-institute-of-chartered-accountants-of-india-pursuant-to-issuance-of-amendments-to-accounting-standards-by-the-mca-september-2016-28-09-2016). Where they differ, AS 10 wins (source hierarchy).
- **Methods:** NCERT §7.6 says straight line and written-down value are the two methods "mandated by law and enforced by professional accounting practice"; AS 10 ¶62, 64 asks that the method reflect the pattern of benefit and lists straight-line, diminishing balance and units of production. AS 10 wins.
- **Cost of a second-hand asset:** NCERT includes initial repairs; AS 10 ¶18 extracts do not list it. Not a conflict; AS 10 ¶17(b) "costs directly attributable" covers it by its terms.
- **Silent:** AS 13 on bank deposits; AS 10 and NCERT on gold and on any small-book exemption from depreciation; NCERT and ICAI extracts on security deposits, RDs, and an asset bought with a loan.
- **AS 1 paragraph numbers** could not be read cleanly from the ICAI page; the disclosure-of-departure rule is cited without a paragraph number.
- **MSME relief from AS 10 confirmed (20 Oct 2026).** ICAI Appendix I (Compendium as on 1 April 2025), (2)(B)(i): "MSMEs may not comply with paragraph 87 relating to encouraged disclosures" — the only AS 10 relaxation; none touches depreciation itself. (2)(A)(i): AS 3 (cash flow statements) does not apply to MSMEs in its entirety. Large non-company entities comply with all Accounting Standards in full ((1)).
- **BoS Foundation Ch. 1, 5, 7, 8 read (20 Oct 2026).** The BoS calls depreciation essential and gives no small-entity exemption, which agrees with NCERT and AS 10 (§1.13). It is silent on gold and on a recurring deposit, and says nothing of a security deposit paid by the book (only one held, §1.14). **BoS is inconsistent with itself on a bank fixed deposit:** Ch. 2 Unit 1 §1.7.1(ii)(a), p. 2.16, calls fixed deposits "real accounts", while the classification table in §1.8, p. 2.29(d), lists "Bank Fixed Deposit" as a *personal* account and an asset. The BoS (Ch. 1 Unit 3 Illustration 4(d)) treats maturity of a fixed deposit as a capital receipt and the interest as revenue, which AS 13/AS 9 (§1.11) do not contradict.

## 4. Answers to the GAPS items this topic settles
- **A1** (things owned carried at cost; purchase, addition, sale at profit/loss, openings): carried at cost including freight, installation, registration (§1.3, NCERT §7.5.1; AS 10 ¶17–18); additions capitalised, repairs expensed (§1.4); sale gain or loss is proceeds less carrying amount (§1.8, AS 10 ¶79); asset against a loan not settled (§3).
- **A3** (fixed and recurring deposits): carried at amount placed, interest is income (§1.11) — by analogy; classification and RD handling not settled (§3).
- **E2** (construction as expense vs asset): for a business, AS 10 says asset when the criteria are met (§1.10); for a trust, not settled here (§3; topic 17 owns it).
- **F-04** and **D-opt-1** (their text was not read; the brief says to answer the topic's questions): the depreciation question is answered in §1.5–1.6 — a charge is the rule, no exemption for a small or cash-basis book was found, and the wording a book must print when it omits it is not settled (§3 row 1).
