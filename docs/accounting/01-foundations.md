# 01 — Accounting foundations

**Status:** Draft, 20 Oct 2026 — for a bookkeeper's review.

This document sets down the groundwork every set of books rests on: the basis on which entries are made, the double entry that gives every amount two sides, the books in which entries are kept, and the way a set of books is opened. It describes bookkeeping practice for Indian households, joint families, their small businesses and trusts or committees, who keep a rokad, a khata and a ledger. It makes no claim that a book kept this way complies with the Accounting Standards or with any statutory format; where the way these books are kept departs from the textbook rule, the rule says so and the report says so too. Later topics are in *02 — Accounts and transactions*, *03 — Entities* and *04 — Year end and reports*.

Plain words used for diary lines: *Money in (Dr)*, *Money out (Cr)*, *Received*, *Spent*, *Paid*, *You will get*, *You will give*, *Cash in hand*, *Bank*, *Opening balance*, *Own money* (capital), *Taken for home* (drawings), *Thing owned*, *Deposit*, *Loan*, *Person / shop*, *Income*, *Expense*, *What I have*, *What I owe*, *Total for the year*.

## 1. Basis of accounting

The basis says *when* an entry is made. The textbooks teach two: the cash basis and the accrual basis. Every book in this series is kept on a third, the khata basis, described in 1.3 as a stated and disclosed departure from the accrual basis the textbooks assume.

### 1.1 The two textbook bases: cash and accrual
On the **cash basis** an entry is made only when money is received or paid, not when the receipt or payment falls due. Rent for December paid in January is entered in January; a credit sale made in January and paid in April is entered in April. Profit is the money received less the money paid in the period. On the **accrual (mercantile) basis** revenues and costs are recognised when they are earned or incurred, and recorded in the period to which they relate, whether or not money has moved; this creates receivables, payables, advances and prepaid items, carried by year-end adjusting entries. NCERT calls the cash basis simple but "inappropriate for most organisations"; AS 1 lists accrual as one of three fundamental assumptions.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Financial Accounting I, ch. 2 §2.4 "Basis of Accounting" (pp. 36–37). ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module 1 ch. 1 unit 2, accrual concept (d) and the timing table that follows; ch. 2 unit 4 "Adjusting entries". AS 1 ¶10(c); ICAI *Framework for the Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements* (2000) ¶22.

**Example.** Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹8,000, entered on 5 January for December's rent (cash basis: nothing is entered in December). Diary: "5 January: Paid ₹8,000 rent for December, cash." On the accrual basis the same rent, unpaid at 31 December, would be entered on 31 December: Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Outstanding Rent A/c ₹8,000.

### 1.2 No textbook names a mixed basis
AS 1, the Framework, NCERT and ICAI BoS name two bases only. None defines or permits a "mixed" or "hybrid" basis. What ICAI BoS does describe is incomplete-records practice: a small business keeps a cash book and personal accounts and finds its result by comparing capital at two dates — which BoS calls "a system of incomplete or improper records", not a basis. A university course summary of B.M. Lall Nigam says "most Indian traders follow cash basis of accounting" and enter credit sales straight to the customer's account; a management-college course PDF describes Gujarati books that also made year-end adjustments. Both are secondary.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 2 §2.4. ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module II ch. 9 §1–§2 (pp. 9.3–9.5). AS 1 ¶10. Shivaji University CDOE IKS course, unit 1 §a (summarising Nigam, Abacus 22(2), 1986 — the paper itself not read). CESS management course PDF "Journey of Indian Accounting Practices", part 2 (secondary).

**Example.** Dr Sunita A/c ₹300 (no credit side recorded anywhere). Diary: "Sunita owes ₹300 for bread and milk."

### 1.3 The khata basis — the basis every book uses
Every book is kept on the **khata basis**:

1. A receipt or payment of money is recorded **when the money moves**.
2. An amount due to or from a named person — a credit sale or purchase, rent charged, an advance, a loan — is recorded **when it arises**, on that person's account. A wage or salary is not such an amount: it is entered when it is paid (rule 1), in the month it is paid, with any advance recovered in the same entry (*02*, salary advance).
3. **Nothing is recorded for the passage of time alone.** There is no accrued, prepaid or outstanding item, no depreciation and no closing stock — unless the book opts into year-end adjustments (1.4).
4. The basis is **stated on every report** (1.5).

The khata basis is the practice of a rokad and a khata already: the rokad records money when it moves, and the khata holds what each person owes or is owed. It is a cash basis for money plus a personal-account record for credit, with no estimates of time. It is not the accrual basis, because rule 3 omits the entries that match cost to period; and it is not the pure cash basis, because rule 2 records a debt when it arises rather than when it is settled. AS 1 ¶27 provides the way to depart from accrual honestly: if a fundamental assumption is not followed, the fact must be disclosed. The khata basis is such a departure, and it is disclosed on every report; the standard does not say that such a book therefore complies with it. One basis for every book — household, joint family, shop, contractor, farm, truck, landlord, partnership, trust — means the same entries mean the same thing in every book. Where a book's owners want accrual, they opt in (1.4); a partnership with no stated basis is on the khata basis, and the partners may opt in for their firm.

**Source:** AS 1 ¶27; NCERT ch. 2 §2.4 — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-01-1, O-01-2, O-01-3, O-01-4): this is how the intended users already keep rokad and khata, and it gives a live position without year-end estimates. Wages in the month paid: owner ruling, 21 Oct 2026 — the month's total then agrees with the money that left the cash box and the bank; a wage still owed at year end is an outstanding expense, made only by a book that opts in (1.4). No source read names a basis for a household, a joint family or a partnership; for the household AS 1 ¶27 applies by analogy, and for the partnership no source says accrual is assumed.

**Example.** Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹8,000 on 5 January; Dr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹10,000 on 31 March for goods sold on credit; Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹10,000 on 10 April when Kapoor pays. Diary: "5 January: Paid ₹8,000 rent, cash." / "31 March: Sold goods ₹10,000 to Kapoor Traders; You will get ₹10,000." / "10 April: Received ₹10,000 from Kapoor Traders." Nothing is written on 31 March for insurance of ₹12,000 paid on 1 October: the whole ₹12,000 was spent when it was paid.

### 1.4 Year-end adjustments are an opt-in, never the default
A business book (shop, contractor, farm, truck, landlord, partnership) or a trust or committee may opt into the four year-end adjustments of the accrual basis: outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, income earned but not received or received in advance, and the use of an asset over time (depreciation), with a closing stock figure where goods are bought for sale. A household or joint-family book stays on the khata basis. Until a book opts in, none is made. ICAI BoS describes cash-basis profit as cash received less cash paid, with no adjustments. A book that has opted in says so on its reports and applies the adjustments every year (1.6). *04 — Year end and reports* describes how the adjustments are made.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module 1 ch. 1 unit 2, p. 1.42 (cash-basis profit has no adjustments); NCERT Class 11 Part I ch. 4 §4.6 item 2 (adjusting entries, on the accrual footing); AS 1 ¶27 — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-19-4, and O-17-3 for the trust). The household and joint family kept out: owner ruling, 21 Oct 2026 — the rulings split on it (O-19-4 and O-09-1 say a business book; O-08-5 and O-09-4 assume a household may), and a household's own reckoning is money in and money out.

**Example.** Insurance ₹12,000 paid on 1 October, books closed 31 December, ₹3,000 used. Default: Dr Insurance A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹12,000 stands as it is. Opted in: also, on 31 December, Dr Prepaid Insurance A/c ₹9,000 · Cr Insurance A/c ₹9,000. Diary: "Default: Spent ₹12,000 on insurance — all of it this year's expense." / "Opted in: ₹9,000 of that is for next year, so it is carried forward."

### 1.5 The basis is stated on every report
AS 1 requires all significant accounting policies to be disclosed (¶24), normally in one place (¶25, explained at ¶18–¶20), and a departure from the fundamental assumptions to be disclosed (¶27). A statement that says nothing is read as going concern, consistent and accrual; a khata-basis book departs from that and must say so. Every report therefore carries one plain line in its head, for example: "Kept on money paid and received, and what people owe; no year-end adjustments." A book that has opted into the adjustments says that instead. A change of policy — a book opting in, or stopping — is disclosed in the year of the change, with its effect on the year's result in rupees (*04 — Year end and reports*, standing notes).

**Source:** AS 1 ¶24–¶25 with ¶18–¶20, ¶22, ¶26, ¶27; NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.10; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module 1 ch. 1 unit 2, consistency concept (k) (p. 1.50) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-20-1) for the wording and its place in the head.

**Example.** Head of a statement: "Kept on money paid and received, and what people owe; no year-end adjustments." Diary: "This book writes money when it moves, and what a person owes when it begins."

### 1.6 Going concern, consistency and the period
AS 1 ¶9–10 names going concern ("continuing in operation for the foreseeable future") and consistency (policies the same from one period to the next). NCERT's going-concern example: a computer of ₹50,000 with a five-year life is charged ₹10,000 a year because the business continues. On the khata basis there is no such charge unless the book opts in (1.4); the whole ₹50,000 stays a thing owned, and its cost is not spread. Consistency does not forbid a change of policy, but the change is disclosed with its probable effect. The accounting period is normally one year, 1 April to 31 March in India; a shorter period is allowed, for example when a book is begun mid-year (4.10).

**Source:** AS 1 ¶9–10, ¶22, ¶26 (a change of policy and its effect); Framework ¶22–24; NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.3 (pp. 28–29), §2.2.4, §2.2.10 (pp. 33–34); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module 1 ch. 1 unit 2, going-concern (f) and periodicity (c).

**Example.** Dr Computer A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹50,000. Textbook accrual then adds each year Dr Depreciation A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Computer A/c ₹10,000; the khata basis does not. Diary: "Bought a computer ₹50,000, paid cash. (Opted-in book only: each year ₹10,000 of it is counted as used.)"

### 1.7 Prudence: no estimate for doubtful amounts
AS 1 ¶17(a): profits are not anticipated but recognised when realised, and known liabilities and losses are provided for. NCERT §2.2.11 gives a provision for doubtful debts as an example, for books that carry debtors. On the khata basis no estimate is made: no book makes a provision for doubtful debts, whether or not it has opted into year-end adjustments (1.4). A balance that may not come back stays on the person's account until it is known not to come, and is then written off as an expense (see *02 — Accounts and transactions*, Persons). NCERT warns that deliberate understatement creates "secret reserves".

**Source:** AS 1 ¶17(a); Framework ¶37; NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.11 (p. 34); ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2, conservatism — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-01-6): an estimate has no place on the khata basis. The textbook rule departed from here is the provision itself.

**Example.** Ramesh owes ₹5,000 and is declared insolvent: Dr Written Off A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Ramesh A/c ₹5,000. Diary: "Written off ₹5,000 Ramesh cannot pay — an expense." Nothing is set aside beforehand for Ramesh's balance.

### 1.8 Substance over form
AS 1 ¶17(b): transactions are accounted for by their substance, not merely their legal form. The Framework's case: property whose rights and benefits have passed to the buyer, while the documents and legal formalities are still pending, is the buyer's. The book enters what has happened in fact, not only what the papers yet say. (Money received before the goods are supplied is an amount owed to the payer, not income; that is a matter of timing, rule 2 of 1.3, not of substance over form.)

**Source:** AS 1 ¶17(b); Framework ¶35. The advance as a liability: ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2 timing table (p. 1.42).

**Example.** A shop is bought for ₹8,00,000, paid in full and taken into use on 1 June; the registry is due in August: Dr Shop A/c ₹8,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹8,00,000 on 1 June, a thing owned from that day, not an advance to the seller. Diary: "Bought the shop ₹8,00,000, Paid from Bank; in use from 1 June, registry in August — Thing owned."

### 1.9 Materiality
AS 1 ¶17(c): items are shown separately if knowing them could influence a user's decisions. Small items such as stationery are charged in full to the year of purchase, whether or not used up.

**Source:** AS 1 ¶17(c); Framework ¶30; NCERT ch. 2 §2.2.12 (pp. 34–35); ICAI BoS ch. 1 unit 2 (l).

**Example.** Dr Stationery A/c ₹300 · Cr Cash A/c ₹300. Diary: "Spent ₹300 on pens and paper — all this year's expense."

### 1.10 The basis in different kinds of book
Sole traders and partnerships are taught accrual for final accounts; small ones in practice keep cash books and personal accounts. A trust or committee produces a Receipts and Payments Account, "a summary of the cash book" that "is not based on the accrual system", and an Income and Expenditure Account that follows accrual with adjustments for outstanding and advance items. Every trust or committee book produces all three statements — Receipts & Payments, Income & Expenditure and the position (Balance Sheet) — on the khata basis. The adjustments it may opt into (1.4) — outstanding and advance items, and depreciation with the fund released in step with it — change only the second and the third; Receipts & Payments is the cash book summarised and never changes (see *03 — Entities*, Trusts). NCERT teaches accrual for the sole proprietor; this document follows the khata basis for every book, as ruled.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 2 ch. 8 §2–§3 and ch. 9 §1–§3; NCERT Class 11 Part I ch. 2 §2.4 (pp. 36–37) and Part II ch. 9 §9.1–§9.6 (accrual and the adjustments); NCERT Class 12 Part I ch. 1 §1.4 (partners' dealings through capital accounts; names no basis) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-17-3, O-17-9) for the trust's statements and the fund release.

**Example.** A society receives ₹1,200 subscriptions, ₹300 of it for next year: Dr Cash A/c ₹1,200 · Cr Subscriptions A/c ₹1,200; Receipts & Payments shows the ₹1,200 in full. Diary: "Members paid ₹1,200 subscriptions. Money in ₹1,200." Whether ₹900 or ₹1,200 counts as income this year depends on whether the book opted into year-end adjustments.

## 2. Double entry and kinds of account

### 2.1 Every amount has two sides, and the sides are equal
Every transaction has two effects — receiving a benefit and giving a benefit — so it touches at least two accounts, and the total debited equals the total credited. A debit is an entry on the left of an account, a credit on the right. Double entry is called a complete system because both aspects are recorded; a trial balance checks the arithmetic. It can be kept "by big as well as small organisations".

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Financial Accounting I, ch. 2 §2.3 (p. 36), ch. 3 §3.3 (pp. 53–54); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.1–§1.2.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Rohit's Own Money A/c ₹5,00,000. Diary: "Rohit put ₹5,00,000 cash into the business. Money in (Dr) ₹5,00,000; Own money (Cr)."

### 2.2 The accounting equation
Assets equal liabilities plus capital: A = L + C; so A − L = C. Resources equal the claims of those who financed them; the owner's claim is capital, outsiders' claims are liabilities. Every transaction leaves the equation in balance, except when there are errors.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 3 §3.2 (pp. 50–52); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.6.

**Example.** Dr Furniture A/c ₹60,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹60,000. Diary: "Bought furniture ₹60,000, paid from Bank. What I have is the same: one Thing owned up, Bank down."

### 2.3 Five types of account
All accounts fall into five types: **asset** (what I have), **liability** (what I owe), **capital** (*Own money*), **income** and **expense**. An increase in an asset or an expense is debited; an increase in a liability, in capital or in income is credited; a decrease is the reverse. A trust's equivalent of capital is its *Fund*. Capital is its own type, not a liability: NCERT §3.3.1 and the ICAI BoS table list it separately, though BoS's own modern-approach worked example labels it "Liability"; this document follows the table.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 3 §3.3.1 (p. 54); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.8 — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-02-1) between the two BoS treatments.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹5,000. Dr Wages A/c ₹1,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹1,000. Dr Cash A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Loan from Kapoor Traders A/c ₹20,000. Diary: "Received ₹5,000, goods sold for cash — Income." / "Paid ₹1,000 wages — Expense." / "Received ₹20,000 as a Loan from Kapoor Traders — What I owe."

### 2.4 Natural balance of each type
An asset, an expense and drawings normally carry a debit balance; a liability, capital and income a credit balance. Assets, liabilities and capital are balanced and carried forward; income and expense accounts are not balanced but closed at the year end into the result (see *04 — Year end and reports*).

**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.8 and unit 2 §2.4; NCERT ch. 3 §3.5.1 (p. 75).

**Example.** Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹55,000 · Cr Sumit Traders A/c ₹55,000, then Dr Sumit Traders A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹20,000: Sumit Traders shows a credit balance of ₹35,000. Diary: "Bought ₹55,000 goods from Sumit Traders on credit; paid ₹20,000; You will give ₹35,000."

### 2.5 A sole owner's drawings are a separate account, closed to Own money at year end
In a book with one owner — a proprietor's business, including a business a person or a family owns, and a household — what the owner takes out for personal use is entered to a separate **Drawings** account (*Taken for home*), debited for each withdrawal, and closed to capital at year end. It is never an expense, and capital is not debited directly. NCERT's answer key places Drawings under Expense; this document follows ICAI BoS and NCERT Class 11, which keep a separate account.

Two kinds of book differ. In a **partnership** a partner's drawings are debited straight to his capital account (fluctuating capital) or his current account (fixed capital), and need no separate closing; this is NCERT Class 12's method (Fig. 1.1–1.2), set out in *03 — Entities* (partnership, drawings). In a **joint family's** book, what a sub-family takes goes to that sub-family's running account (*03 — Entities*, joint family).

**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.8 and Foundation ch. 7 unit 1 p. 7.19 (separate account closed to capital); NCERT Class 11 Part I ch. 4 §4.6(4); NCERT Class 12 Part I ch. 1 §1.4 and Fig. 1.1–1.2 (pp. 6–7; a partner's drawings debited in the current or capital account) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-02-3, O-13-1), whose scope is the sole owner, household and joint family; the partnership follows 03 (O-14-4).

**Example.** Dr Drawings A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹3,000. Diary: "Took ₹3,000 for home. Money out (Cr); Taken for home."

### 2.6 Personal, real and nominal accounts; the golden rules
The traditional classification divides accounts into **personal** (persons: natural, artificial such as a company or club, and representative), **real** (things owned: land, cash, investments) and **nominal** (expenses, losses, gains, income). The golden rules: personal — debit the receiver, credit the giver; real — debit what comes in, credit what goes out; nominal — debit expenses and losses, credit income and gains. A bank is treated as a personal account (the bank is an artificial person), although BoS elsewhere lists cash at bank as real; in the five types it is an asset, or a liability when overdrawn. NCERT Class 11 teaches the five-type rules and does not state the three traditional rules. This classification is for a bookkeeper's reading; the diary words need not use it.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.7.1, §1.7.2 (printed p. 2.17) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-02-2): the bank is personal under the golden rules, following the BoS worked example (§1.7.2); BoS §1.7.1 itself calls cash at bank real, so the sources split and the ruling chose.

**Example.** Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹55,000 · Cr Sumit Traders A/c ₹55,000 (nominal debited as an expense; personal credited as the giver). Dr Ramesh A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹10,000 (receiver debited; what goes out credited). Diary: "Bought goods ₹55,000 on credit from Sumit Traders." / "Paid Ramesh ₹10,000 cash in advance — You will get it back or in goods."

### 2.7 How the two classifications map
Neither textbook prints a mapping; this is a reading of both. A debtor is a personal account and an asset; a creditor or lender is personal and a liability; capital and drawings are personal-representative and capital; outstanding expense is a liability and prepaid an asset (opted-in books only); a real account is an asset; a nominal expense is an expense and a nominal gain is income.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.7.1, §1.8; ch. 1 unit 2 timing table; NCERT ch. 3 §3.2–3.3 — the mapping is ours, not printed in either source.

**Example.** Dr Bank A/c ₹50,00,000 · Cr Own Money A/c ₹50,00,000: traditional — the bank receives, the owner gives; modern — an asset up, capital up. Diary: "Started with ₹50,00,000 in the Bank."

### 2.8 A loan to or from a relative sits on the person's page
The person's own running account carries money given and still to come back on the debit side (*You will get*) and money taken on the credit side (*You will give*). A loan that carries interest or a repayment schedule gets its own account, *Loan to – {person}* or *Loan from – {person}*, shown beside the person's running account on the same page; this keeps an interest-bearing loan from hiding among everyday udhaar. Deposits, retention money and advances given to be spent for the book (including a salary advance) are likewise kept in their own accounts on the person's page (see *02 — Accounts and transactions*). A trade advance — paid to a supplier ahead of the goods, or received from a customer ahead of them — is not: it stays in the person's running account, on the side it falls.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-02-4): matches the per-person deposit ruling of 13 Oct 2026; ICAI BoS §1.7.1 says only that personal accounts relate to persons.

**Example.** Dr Loan to – Manjit A/c ₹40,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹40,000. Diary: "Lent Manjit ₹40,000 by Bank transfer, with interest agreed. Money out (Cr); You will get ₹40,000."

### 2.9 A compound entry
When more than two accounts are affected the entry is compound, and total debits equal total credits.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 3 §3.4.1 (pp. 62–63); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.9.2.

**Example.** Dr Office Furniture A/c ₹25,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Modern Furniture A/c ₹20,000. Diary: "Bought furniture ₹25,000 from Modern Furniture; Paid ₹5,000 cash, You will give ₹20,000."

### 2.10 Goods bought for sale
NCERT and ICAI BoS debit goods bought for sale to a Purchases account (here called *Goods bought for sale*), treated as an expense, not to a Goods asset account. On the khata basis no closing stock is carried unless the book opts in (1.4); goods on the shelf when a book opens are entered to this same account (4.6). How stock is counted or valued is outside this document.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 3 §3.4.1 (p. 62) and worked transaction 5 (p. 56).

**Example.** Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Govind Traders A/c ₹30,000. Diary: "Bought goods ₹30,000 from Govind Traders on credit; You will give ₹30,000."

## 3. Books of account

### 3.1 Every entry rests on a document, or on a note where there is none
A transaction is entered when the money moves or the due arises (1.3), and the document behind it is kept: cash memo, bill, receipt, pay-slip or pass-book. Where there is no document — daily labour, a small cash expense, money handed to a relative, a difference found when the cash is counted — the entry is still made, and carries a note of who was paid or who paid, and why; the note is its voucher. NCERT's objectivity concept: each transaction has a verifiable voucher, and where petty items have no document a voucher is prepared showing the details.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Financial Accounting I, ch. 3 §3.1 (p. 47: "voucher may be prepared showing the necessary details" for petty expenses) and ch. 2 §2.2.13; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.5 (p. 2.10).

**Example.** Dr Computer A/c ₹35,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹35,000, cash memo kept. Dr Wages A/c ₹800 · Cr Cash A/c ₹800, no slip, note: "two labourers, unloading the truck, one day". Diary: "Bought a computer ₹35,000, Paid cash; cash memo kept." / "Paid ₹800 wages for unloading — no slip, note kept."

### 3.2 The journal and the ledger
The journal is the book of original (first) entry: each transaction in date order, with date, the accounts, a narration and the amount. The ledger is the principal book: every account on its own page, with a debit and a credit side. Posting copies each journal line to its accounts. The journal is chronological; the ledger is analytical, showing the net position of any account — what a customer owes, what a supplier is owed — which the journal cannot.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 3 §3.4, §3.5, §3.6 and "Distinction between Journal and Ledger" (pp. 60–62, 63, 73–75); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 1 §1.9 (pp. 2.18–2.19) and unit 2 §2.1–2.3 (p. 2.55).

**Example.** Journal: Dr Rent A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹8,000. Ledger: Rent A/c, debit side ₹8,000; Cash A/c, credit side ₹8,000. Diary: "Paid ₹8,000 rent, cash — written once in date order, then once on the Rent page and once on the Cash page."

### 3.3 The Day Book: the rokad, for every kind of entry
The rokad (rojmel) bahi is the book in which a transaction is first entered. In the textbooks the cash book "serves the purpose of both journal as well as the ledger (cash) account", and a course summary says the rojmel "serves the purpose of a cash account". Here the **Day Book** replaces the rokad: every entry of every kind, in date order, is written once in it. The Day Book is both cash book and journal for cash entries. An entry that moves no money — a write-off, a transfer between heads — appears in it marked *no money moved*. The textbooks use "day books" only for the group of subsidiary books; no source calls one book of everything a day book, and this document does.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 4 §4.1 (p. 100); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 5 §5.1 (p. 2.108); CESS management course PDF, part 2 — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-03-1): one Day Book replaces the rokad for every kind of entry. A university summary of Nigam (Shivaji) treats the rokad as a cash book only.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹2,000, written once in the Day Book. Diary: "Received ₹2,000 from Kapoor Traders — Money in (Dr). Kapoor's page shows it as received."

### 3.4 The Cash Book is the view of cash and bank
The **Cash Book** records all receipts and payments of cash and bank, starts with the cash and bank balances at the opening of the period and is a view of the Day Book, not a second record. A credit opening balance in the bank column is an overdraft. NCERT: the cash book is kept by "all organisations, big or small"; ICAI BoS: it is both a subsidiary book and a principal book.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 4 §4.1–§4.1.4; ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 5 §5.1 and Summary (pp. 2.108, 2.124–2.125) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-03-1).

**Example.** Opening: Dr Cash ₹2,000 (To Balance b/d); bank column ₹500 on the credit side (overdraft). Diary: "Cash in hand ₹2,000; the Bank is overdrawn by ₹500."

### 3.5 Balancing an account
At the end of a period each personal and real account is totalled on both sides; the difference goes on the shorter side as "Balance c/d" and is brought down as "Balance b/d" at the start of the next. Income and expense accounts are not balanced but closed into the result. A statement for any period shows the opening balance first, then the entries, then the closing balance.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 4 §4.7 (pp. 141–142); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 2 §2.4 (pp. 2.55–2.56).

**Example.** Stationery bought in January: ₹480 opening, ₹800 by cheque, ₹1,280 on credit; By Balance c/d ₹2,560. Diary: "Stationery page: ₹2,560 carried to February."

### 3.6 Entries that move no money: the journal proper
What fits no special book goes to the journal proper: opening, closing, adjusting, rectification and transfer entries, and items such as goods taken by the owner for home. In the bahi-khata the havalamel (journal-proper) entries close and adjust accounts.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 4 introduction and §4.6 (pp. 139–140); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 4 §4.7 (pp. 2.96–2.97); CESS course PDF, part 3 (secondary).

**Example.** Dr Drawings A/c ₹4,000 · Cr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹4,000 (our illustration of "goods withdrawn by the owner for personal use"). Diary: "Took goods worth ₹4,000 for home. *No money moved.*"

### 3.7 What every book keeps: Day Book, Cash Book and ledger
NCERT calls single entry "not a system but a lack of system" and ICAI BoS names three forms of incomplete records: personal accounts only; personal accounts and a cash book; personal accounts, a cash book and some subsidiary books. BoS says profit found this way "is only an estimate", and that the owner's cash book "mixes up business and personal transactions". No source says which minimum is adequate. Every book here keeps a Day Book, a Cash Book and a ledger with an account for every person, every income and expense head and every thing owned; there are no separate sales or purchase books. Full double entry costs the user nothing more and gives the trial balance and the position.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 2 §2.3 (p. 36); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module II ch. 9 §1–§2 (pp. 9.3–9.5) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-03-5).

**Example.** A shop sale ₹300 on credit to Mr A, kept as a bare register line (Dr Mr A ₹300) has no Sales entry; here it is Dr Mr A A/c ₹300 · Cr Sales A/c ₹300. Diary: "Sold bread and milk ₹300 to Mr A; You will get ₹300."

### 3.8 Income and expense heads are posted; a trial balance results
A course summary of Nigam says the traditional book posts no nominal accounts and so yields no profit and loss account or trial balance; the CESS course PDF describes nominal accounts, a rough trial balance (utaro) and closing entries. Neither original was read. Every book here posts its income and expense heads to the ledger and yields a trial balance, because the year's result and the position depend on it.

**Source:** CESS management course PDF, part 2; Shivaji University CDOE IKS course, unit 1 §a (secondary; Nigam not read) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-03-2).

**Example.** Dr Wages A/c ₹100 · Cr Cash A/c ₹100, posted to the Wages page and the Cash page. Diary: "Paid ₹100 wages — Expense."

### 3.9 The bahi-khata vocabulary: Jama and Naam
The bahi-khata books are hard-bound, red-covered, in Mahajani or similar scripts; the left of the page is **Jama** (credit) and the right **Naam** or **Udhar** (debit) — the reverse of the English ledger. One line in the rokad names the *other* account only, on the side that account takes: money received is written Jama ("Shri Rent Khate Jama"), money paid is written Udhar ("Shri Wages Khate Udhar"). The khata bahi is the ledger and the basis of the rough trial balance; entries that move no cash are havalamel. Here **Jama means credit and Naam / Udhar means debit, for vocabulary only**; printed reports keep the modern layout, debit column first, and never the traditional page orientation. Bahi-khata findings rest on secondary course material and are indicative, not confirmed.

**Source:** CESS management course PDF, parts 2–3; Shivaji University CDOE IKS course, unit 1 §a; Sarthaks eConnect, "What are the terminologies used in Mahajani Bahi khata system" (RBSE Class 9 answer; student-contributed) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-03-4): the words help Punjabi and Hindi users, one layout keeps reports readable.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹300 · Cr Rent A/c ₹300: rokad, Jama side, "Shri Rent Khate Jama ₹300". Dr Wages A/c ₹100 · Cr Cash A/c ₹100: rokad, Udhar side, "Shri Wages Khate Udhar ₹100". Diary: "Received ₹300 rent — Money in (Dr)." / "Paid ₹100 wages — Money out (Cr)."

## 4. Opening a set of books

### 4.1 A new business opens with the owner's money
A new business has no earlier balances. Its first entries record what the owner brings in: cash introduced is debited to Cash, money put in the bank to Bank, and the credit is the owner's capital (*Own money*). In a new cash book the amount is written "To Capital Account".

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Financial Accounting I, ch. 3 §3.2–3.3.1 (pp. 51, 55); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 5, three-column cash book point 1 (p. 2.112).

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Rohit's Own Money A/c ₹5,00,000. Diary: "Rohit started the business with ₹5,00,000 cash."

### 4.2 An existing business opens with a statement of what it has and owes
When a business that has run without proper books is brought into books, a statement of affairs is drawn up: assets and liabilities at that date laid out like a balance sheet, with capital as the balancing figure — capital = assets − liabilities. Sources for the figures: the cash book for cash; the pass-book for the bank; the personal ledger or list for what persons owe and are owed; fixed assets from a list of cost and date. ICAI BoS says a statement of affairs is built partly from records and partly from memory and estimate and its valuation is "arbitrary"; the book therefore states the date and where each figure came from.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1 Accounting (Module II, Aug 2026 ed.), ch. 9 §3.1 (pp. 9.6–9.8; design on p. 9.8) and §3.2 (pp. 9.9–9.10).

**Example.** Mr X on 31 March 2021: Building ₹1,00,000, Furniture ₹50,000, Inventory ₹1,20,000, Debtors ₹40,000, Bank ₹70,000, Cash ₹1,200 = ₹3,81,200; Loans ₹1,00,000 and Creditors ₹40,000 = ₹1,40,000; capital (balancing figure) ₹2,41,200. In the opening entry the inventory goes to *Goods bought for sale* (4.6). Diary: "On 31 March 2021 What I have was ₹3,81,200 and What I owe ₹1,40,000, so Own money is ₹2,41,200."

### 4.3 Own money is the balancing figure; no separate Opening Balance account
The opening entry debits each asset, credits each liability in its own account, and credits Own money (capital) with the balancing figure. Everything the book has and owes on the opening day goes in: cash, bank, each person's balance, things owned, and goods on the shelf for sale (4.6). No separate "Opening Balance" account is used: none of the sources read uses one in an opening entry (nor forbids one), and the textbooks credit Capital. For a **household** the equivalent is *Own money* too: what the household has less what it owes on the opening day, the balancing figure of the opening statement.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part II ch. 8 §8.7 (p. 308); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, ch. 7 unit 1 §1.8, Illustration 8 (pp. 7.43–7.44) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-04-1, O-04-2); for the household, ICAI BoS ch. 9 (statement of affairs) by analogy.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹43,000 · Dr Bank A/c ₹2,67,500 · Dr Trade Receivables A/c ₹7,49,500 · Dr Machinery and Equipment A/c ₹6,00,000 · Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹9,00,000 · Cr Mahendra & Sons A/c ₹5,60,000 · Cr Own Money A/c ₹20,00,000 (₹25,60,000 each side). The BoS illustration gives receivables as one total; in this book it is one line per person, each on that person's account (4.4). Diary: "1 April: Cash in hand ₹43,000, Bank ₹2,67,500, ₹7,49,500 You will get, machines ₹6,00,000, goods on the shelf ₹9,00,000; You will give Mahendra & Sons ₹5,60,000; the rest, ₹20,00,000, is Own money."

### 4.4 Opening balances of persons
After the opening entry each person's account starts with a balance brought down on its own side: a person who owes the book as "To Balance b/d" on the debit side (*You will get*), a person the book owes as "By Balance b/d" on the credit side (*You will give*). The brought-down balance and the opening entry are the same amount.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 2 §2.4 (p. 2.56); NCERT ch. 4 §4.7 (p. 141).

**Example.** Dr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹15,500 · Cr Own Money A/c ₹15,500, as a line of the opening entry. Diary: "Opening day: Kapoor Traders owed me ₹15,500 — You will get."

### 4.5 Opening balances of cash and bank
The Cash Book (a view of the cash and bank ledger accounts) starts with the cash and bank balances at the start of the period, written "To Balance b/d". A debit balance in the bank column is a favourable balance; a credit balance is an overdraft, a liability. Cash and bank are ledger accounts like any other, and the Cash Book is the view of them; the Day Book is the first record.

**Source:** NCERT ch. 4 §4.1 (p. 100); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 5, three-column cash book point 1 (p. 2.112), Summary (pp. 2.124–2.125) and p. 2.127.

**Example.** Dr Cash ₹2,000 · bank overdraft ₹500 (credit). Diary: "Opening day: Cash in hand ₹2,000; the Bank is overdrawn by ₹500 — What I owe."

### 4.6 Opening balances of things owned
A fixed asset enters the opening statement at original cost less depreciation to date (written-down value); in a statement of affairs the owner supplies cost and date and an accountant allows reasonable depreciation. On the khata basis the book makes no depreciation of its own (1.3), so a thing owned opens at its cost when the cost is known, and otherwise at the value the owner states on the opening day; the accounts list says which, and the figure then stays unchanged.

Goods on the shelf for sale on the opening day are entered at one rupee figure — the closing-stock figure of an earlier book if there is one, otherwise the figure the owner gives — and are debited to *Goods bought for sale*, not to an asset account: Dr Goods bought for sale · Cr Own money. That is the account a closing-stock figure goes back into on the first day of every later year (*02*, closing stock), so a book opening for the first time and a book continuing from last year open the same way, and the goods are charged against the year in which they are sold. A shop with nothing on the shelf enters nothing. How stock is counted or valued is outside this document.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module II ch. 9 §3.1 (p. 9.8) and §3.2, "Basis of valuation" row (p. 9.10). The khata-basis treatment: none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-04-7): cost is the figure the owner can prove; with no depreciation a written-down value cannot be computed. Opening goods to *Goods bought for sale*: owner ruling, 21 Oct 2026 — everything the book has on the opening day goes in, and the purchase adjustment chosen for closing stock (O-10-2) is the one place stock is carried; leaving opening goods out would let a closing count at the first year end overstate the year's result by the whole opening stock.

**Example.** Furniture bought for ₹50,000 two years ago, cost known, opens as Dr Furniture A/c ₹50,000 against Own money. Diary: "Furniture that cost ₹50,000 is put in at ₹50,000." If the cost is not known and the owner states ₹40,000: Dr Furniture A/c ₹40,000, "Furniture put in at ₹40,000, the value the owner gives." Goods on the shelf worth ₹9,00,000 on the opening day: Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹9,00,000 · Cr Own Money A/c ₹9,00,000. Diary: "Goods on the shelf ₹9,00,000 put in on the first day."

### 4.7 Partners' capital accounts are the opening capital
In a firm every dealing between the partners and the firm goes through the partners' capital accounts. At the firm's opening, its capital is the sum of what the partners bring in. Partners' opening figures **must equal assets less liabilities**; if the agreed figures do not, the partners settle the difference between themselves, in their profit-sharing ratio unless they agree otherwise, before the book opens. No balancing line is made.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Accountancy Part I, ch. 1 §1.4; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Module II ch. 9 §1 and §3.1 (pp. 9.3–9.4, 9.6; capital = assets − liabilities, p. 9.7) — and ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-04-5): no source treats a mismatch; no suspense, consistent with 4.3. The textbooks give no partnership opening entry; the example is built from §1.4.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹3,00,000 · Dr Furniture A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Ramesh's Own Money A/c ₹3,00,000 · Cr Sunita's Own Money A/c ₹1,00,000. Diary: "Ramesh put in ₹3,00,000 cash; Sunita put in furniture worth ₹1,00,000."

### 4.8 A joint family opens with its assets less its liabilities
No source treats a joint family. The family's own money as a whole — its own-money account together with the sub-families' running accounts — opens at the family's assets less its liabilities. Each sub-family's running account opens at the balance the family and that sub-family agree (nil if none), mirrored in the sub-family's own book; the family's own-money account takes what is left.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-04-3): follows the running-account ruling of 13 Oct 2026; no source treats a joint family.

**Example.** The family has ₹6,00,000 and owes ₹1,00,000; the elder son's family's running account stands at ₹20,000 in its favour (more put in than taken), agreed by both books; it sits under the family's own money and is not a debt: Dr Cash A/c ₹6,00,000 · Cr Loan from Bank A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Family Own Money A/c ₹4,80,000 · Cr Elder Son's Family A/c ₹20,000. Diary: "Opening: Cash in hand ₹6,00,000; Loan ₹1,00,000; Own money ₹5,00,000, of which Elder Son's family ₹20,000."

### 4.9 When what a book owes is more than it has
When liabilities exceed assets, Own money is shown as a negative figure on its usual side, with the plain note "owes more than it owns". It is never moved to the assets side. The older "capital deficiency on the assets side" form (seen only in a secondary guide) misleads a lay reader; a negative figure states the fact.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-04-4): ICAI BoS ch. 9 does not say how a deficit is shown.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Loan from Sumit A/c ₹30,000 · Dr Own Money A/c ₹20,000 (a negative Own money of ₹20,000). Diary: "Opening: Cash in hand ₹10,000; Loan ₹30,000; Own money −₹20,000 — owes more than it owns."

### 4.10 The opening date
Opening balances of persons, banks and things are taken on the date the book begins: the first day of the financial year, or the day chosen when a book is started mid-year. The first period runs from that day. ICAI BoS ch. 9 uses two fixed dates; a mid-year start is only a different first date.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-04-6): ICAI BoS ch. 9 uses fixed dates and does not treat a mid-year start.

**Example.** Dr Cash A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Own Money A/c ₹12,000, dated 1 August. Diary: "1 August: began the book with Cash in hand ₹12,000; the first period runs from 1 August."

### 4.11 The opening is checked first
The total of the debit opening balances (assets) must equal the total of the credit opening balances (liabilities and capital); capital is the figure that makes it so. When a trial balance does not agree, "the posting of all the entries including the opening entry should be checked".

**Source:** NCERT ch. 3 §3.2 (pp. 50–51); ICAI BoS Module 1 ch. 2 unit 6 §6.4 item (viii) (p. 2.138).

**Example.** Debits ₹36,500 (Furniture ₹15,000, Debtors ₹15,500, Bank ₹5,000, Cash ₹1,000) equal credits ₹36,500 (Own Money ₹16,500, Loan ₹5,000, Creditors ₹15,000). Diary: "What I have, ₹36,500, equals What I owe plus Own money."
