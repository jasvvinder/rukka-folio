# 02 — Accounts and transactions

**Status:** Draft, 20 Oct 2026 — for a bookkeeper's review.

Every entry moves an amount from one account to another, between accounts the person has opened, each of a kind in the table below; the person decides which accounts an entry uses. This document gives the kinds of account and shows common transactions as worked entries in the books of an Indian household, a joint family, a small business (shop, contractor, farm, truck, landlord, partnership) or a trust or committee: cash and bank, people who owe or are owed, advances, loans, things owned and deposits, goods bought for sale, corrections, and entries that move no money. A case not shown here is entered the same way, from one account to another; it needs no rule of its own. It describes bookkeeping practice for people who keep a rokad, a khata and a ledger; it does not claim that a book kept this way complies with the Accounting Standards or any statutory format. The basis of recording (the *khata basis*), the books themselves, entity-specific rules and the year-end and reports are in the other documents: *01 — Accounting foundations*, *03 — Entities*, *04 — Year end and reports*.

**The khata basis, in one paragraph.** Money is recorded when it moves. An amount due to or from a named person (a credit sale or purchase, rent charged, an advance, a loan) is recorded when it arises. Nothing is recorded for time alone: no accrued or prepaid items, no depreciation, no closing stock, no provision for doubtful debts — unless a business book, or a trust or committee, opts into year-end adjustments (a household or joint-family book does not), which *04 — Year end and reports* describes. Every rule below is consistent with this, and each says where an optional year-end variant exists.

## Kinds of account

| Plain word | Standard type | Natural side | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | Asset | Dr | Cash in hand; the shop till; *Petty cash*, the float held by a petty cashier (3.7) |
| Gollak | Asset | Dr | Donation box of a gurdwara, mandir or committee, until counted and banked |
| Bank | Asset | Dr (Cr when overdrawn) | Savings, current and salary accounts; an overdraft on a current account |
| Wallet | Asset | Dr | UPI wallet, FASTag balance, prepaid card |
| Credit card | Liability | Cr | A card issued by a bank; the unpaid bill |
| Cash-credit / KCC loan | Liability | Cr | A Kisan Credit Card or cash-credit limit kept as its own account at the bank |
| Person / shop | Asset or Liability (changes side) | Dr = You will get; Cr = You will give | Customer, supplier, relative, tenant, contractor, member |
| Advance | Asset | Dr | *Advance – {person}*: money handed to a driver, foreman, caretaker, sewadar or member to spend for the book, or a salary advance |
| Deposit held | Liability | Cr | *Deposit held – {person}*: a tenant's security deposit |
| Deposit paid | Asset | Dr | *Deposit paid – {person}*: security paid to a landlord or supplier |
| Retention held | Asset | Dr | *Retention held by – {party}*: money a customer or contractor holds back until work is accepted |
| Loan | Liability (from) or Asset (to) | Cr (from) or Dr (to) | *Loan from – {person}*, *Loan to – {person}*, a bank term loan, a vehicle loan |
| Thing owned | Asset | Dr | Truck, tractor, machine, land, building, gold, furniture |
| Deposit | Asset | Dr | Fixed deposit, recurring deposit, other investment placed with a bank or institution |
| Income head | Income | Cr | Sales, rent received, interest received, donations, commission received |
| Expense head | Expense | Dr | Goods bought for sale, wages, rent, repairs, interest paid, household costs |
| Written off | Expense | Dr | *Written off*, or a named head such as *Rent written off* |
| Cash short / over | Expense when short (Dr); Income when over (Cr) | Dr or Cr | The difference between the cash counted and the cash book |
| Not yet sorted | Holding account | Dr or Cr | A bank-statement line not yet given its account; sorted before the year closes (7.5) |

Own money (capital) and Drawings belong to the owner's side of the book and are described in *03 — Entities*.

**One running account per person.** Every person or shop has a single running account. Sales, purchases, money given and money taken all go into it, and its balance changes side: on the debit side it reads *You will get*, on the credit side *You will give*. Four things are the exceptions, and each gets its own account on that person's page, shown beside the running account and never netted into it: a **deposit** (held or paid), **retention money**, an **advance for spending**, and an **interest-bearing loan** (or one with a repayment schedule). Netting them in would make money that is not yet due look overdue, or hide a loan inside everyday udhaar. The rules in sections 2, 3, 4 and 5 below state each exception.

ICAI Board of Studies (BoS) material is study material, not a pronouncement; NCERT is the Class 11 Accountancy textbook. A rule marked *none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026* has no external source; the reason is given in one line.

## 1. Cash and bank

### 1.1 Cash and bank are ledger accounts; the cash book is a view of them
Every receipt of cash goes on the money-in side and every payment on the money-out side, starting from the balance at the start of the period. Each is entered once in the day book, which is the first record; cash and each bank are ledger accounts, and the cash book is a view of those accounts, with a column for cash and a column for each bank.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1, §4.1.1, §4.1.2 [1].
**Example.** `Dr Cash A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Ramesh A/c ₹5,000` (cash received from Ramesh; only Ramesh's account is also posted).
Diary: *Money in ₹5,000 (Dr) — Received from Ramesh in cash.*

### 1.2 Cash is never below nil; a bank balance may be
The cash balance is always on the debit side, because cash paid out cannot exceed what came in. A bank balance may fall on the credit side when the account is overdrawn (see 1.6).
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.2, §4.1.4 [1].
**Example.** Cash opening ₹8,000, received ₹4,000, paid ₹9,500: balance `Dr ₹2,500`. A bank book closing `Cr ₹3,200` means the account is overdrawn by ₹3,200.
Diary: *Cash in hand ₹2,500.* / *Bank: I owe the bank ₹3,200.*

### 1.3 A move between two of the book's own accounts is a Transfer
Cash paid into the bank, and cash drawn from the bank for the business's own cash box, move money between two of the book's own accounts. They are entered once, as a Transfer, and are posted to no person or head. Every other move between the book's own accounts is a Transfer too: a wallet loaded (1.9), a petty-cash float handed over (3.7), a card bill paid (1.7), a KCC repaid (4.5), a deposit placed or returned (5.9, 5.10). In the Cash Book only a Transfer between cash and a bank is a contra entry, shown on both sides; a Transfer to or from a wallet, a card, a loan or a deposit is a payment or a receipt of the cash or bank. A cheque received on one day and banked on a later day is Money in (cash) on the day of receipt, and a Transfer to the bank on the day it is banked.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.2 ("contra entry") [1]; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 5 §5.2 point 4, p. 2.109 [11].
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹20,000`; and `Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹10,000`.
Diary: *Transfer ₹20,000 from Cash in hand to Bank.* / *Transfer ₹10,000 from Bank to Cash in hand — for the till.*

### 1.4 Cash drawn from the bank for private use is Drawings, not a Transfer
A withdrawal for the owner's or the family's own use is not moved between the book's own accounts; it leaves the book. It is Drawings. A Transfer is only a move between two of the book's own accounts (1.3).
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 5 §5.2 p. 2.109 [11]; Ch. 9 §4.4 p. 9.22 [18].
**Example.** `Dr Drawings A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹10,000`.
Diary: *Drawings ₹10,000 from Bank — for house expenses.*

### 1.5 What the bank does without being asked goes into the bank book
Charges, interest debited, standing-instruction payments and direct debits are money out of the bank book. Interest, dividends and direct deposits the bank credits are money in. A customer's cheque that comes back unpaid is entered as money out of the bank against the customer, restoring what he owed before.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.2 [1]; ICAI BoS Ch. 3 §5 cause (vi), p. 3.10 [13].
**Example.** `Dr Bank Charges A/c ₹120 · Cr Bank A/c ₹120`. Dishonour: `Dr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹12,000`.
Diary: *Money out ₹120 (Cr Bank) — Bank charges: the bank took service charges.* / *Kapoor Traders' cheque for ₹12,000 bounced — You will get ₹12,000 from Kapoor Traders again.*

### 1.6 An overdraft or cash credit on a current account is the bank account running a credit balance
An overdraft, or a cash credit operated through the current account, is not a separate loan account. It is the same bank account standing on the credit side. The sanctioned limit is not an entry. In the position, a credit balance shows under *What I owe* as a short-term loan. A Kisan Credit Card or cash-credit limit that the bank keeps as a separate loan account is different: see 4.5.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.2 [1]; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 5 §5.2 p. 2.110 [11]; Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.6 p. 7.39 [16].
**Example.** Bank book closes `Cr ₹8,000`; the bank charges ₹160 interest: `Dr Interest on Overdraft A/c ₹160 · Cr Bank A/c ₹160`.
Diary: *Bank: I owe the bank ₹8,000 (What I owe).* / *Money out ₹160 (Cr Bank) — Interest on overdraft: the bank charged interest.*

### 1.7 A credit card is a liability of its own; paying the bill is a Transfer
Each card has its own account. A spend on the card is not money out of the bank: it credits the card and debits the expense or the thing bought. Paying the bill is a Transfer from the bank to the card, which brings the card account down.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-05-1): the sources treat a card only from the seller's side; an amount owed to the card issuer is a liability.
**Example.** `Dr Fuel A/c ₹3,000 · Cr HDFC Card A/c ₹3,000`; later `Dr HDFC Card A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹3,000`.
Diary: *Spent ₹3,000 on Fuel — on HDFC card.* / *Transfer ₹3,000 from Bank to HDFC card — bill paid.*

### 1.8 A sale taken on a customer's card is a cash sale; the bank's commission is an expense
Seen from the seller, a card sale is a cash sale: the bank credits the seller's account and takes a commission, which comes out of the bank at once as an expense, *Card commission*.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 5 §5.5, pp. 2.117–2.118 [11].
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹5,000`; commission at 2%: `Dr Card Commission A/c ₹100 · Cr Bank A/c ₹100`.
Diary: *Money in ₹5,000 (Dr Bank) — Sale on a customer's card.* / *Money out ₹100 (Cr Bank) — Card commission: the bank's charge.*

### 1.9 A wallet is a money account of its own
A wallet is an asset that behaves like cash. Loading it is a Transfer from the bank or cash; spending from it is a payment out of the wallet.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-05-2): no source treats a wallet; it behaves as money the book holds.
**Example.** `Dr Wallet A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹2,000`; then `Dr Conveyance A/c ₹150 · Cr Wallet A/c ₹150`.
Diary: *Transfer ₹2,000 from Bank to Wallet.* / *Spent ₹150 on Conveyance — from Wallet.*

### 1.10 Every bank account is checked against its statement each month and at year end
Each month the book is compared with the bank's statement. What the bank did that the book lacks (charges, interest, direct credits and debits) is entered in the book. What remains is only timing: cheques written but not yet presented, cheques banked but not yet credited. These are listed, and the statement is dated at its head. At year end the check is mandatory, so the closing bank balance is right.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 3 §2–§3 p. 3.4 (monthly or quarterly) and §7.2.1 p. 3.25 (the book must be adjusted at year end) [13]; NCERT Part I ch. 5 §5.1–5.2 [2]. Monthly, rather than quarterly, is ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-05-4): importing statement lines makes it cheap, and it is part of the month-end check.
**Example.** Book balance ₹40,000; statement shows charges ₹300 and an insurance premium ₹1,500 paid on standing instruction, not in the book; a cheque of ₹2,000 not yet presented. Enter `Dr Bank Charges A/c ₹300 · Dr Insurance A/c ₹1,500 · Cr Bank A/c ₹1,800` (book now ₹38,200); ₹38,200 + ₹2,000 cheque not presented = ₹40,200 on the statement.
Diary: *Bank took ₹300 charges and paid ₹1,500 insurance; written into my bank book. The only difference left is the ₹2,000 cheque not yet banked by the shop.*

## 2. Persons: debtors and creditors

### 2.1 One running account per person
A person who is sometimes a customer and sometimes a supplier has one account, not two. Sales to him, goods sent back to him and money given to him are debited; purchases from him, goods he sends back and money taken from him are credited. The balance is what remains, shown as *You will get* or *You will give* by its side. Deposits, retention money, advances for spending and interest-bearing loans are the exceptions (see the opening of this document).
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.7, p. 146 ("Ramneek's Account") [1]; §4.3, p. 127 (a purchase return debits the supplier) [1]. Dual-role single account ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-06-1): the khata the users already keep.
**Example.** `Dr Ramesh A/c ₹4,140 · Cr Sales A/c ₹4,140`; return `Dr Sales Return A/c ₹440 · Cr Ramesh A/c ₹440`; payment `Dr Cash A/c ₹3,700 · Cr Ramesh A/c ₹3,700`. Balance nil.
Diary: *Gave on credit ₹4,140 to Ramesh (You will get). He returned ₹440 and Received ₹3,700 — account clear.*

### 2.2 A payment ahead to a supplier is the supplier's account standing on the debit side
Money paid to a supplier before the goods arrive is an asset (a trade advance) until the goods are received. Because the person has one running account, the advance is simply that account on the debit side: *You will get* goods or the money back. When the goods arrive the purchase is credited to the same account and the balance falls.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 1 Unit 2 pp. 1.38–1.39 [8]; Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.6 p. 7.39 and Illustration 6 pp. 7.40–7.41 [16]. Single-account form ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-06-2).
**Example.** `Dr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹20,000`; goods arrive: `Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹20,000`.
Diary: *Paid ₹20,000 to Kapoor Traders ahead (You will get goods).* / *Took on credit ₹20,000 of goods from Kapoor Traders — balance nil.*

### 2.3 A customer's advance is the customer's account standing on the credit side
Money received from a customer before delivery is a liability until the goods go out. On the person's single account it is a credit balance, *You will give* goods or the money back. On delivery the sale is debited to the same account.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 1 Unit 2 p. 1.38 [8]; Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.6 p. 7.39; exercise 4 and solution pp. 7.66, 7.73 [16]. Single-account form ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-06-3).
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Ramesh A/c ₹30,000`; goods delivered: `Dr Ramesh A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹30,000`.
Diary: *Money in ₹30,000 from Ramesh ahead (You will give goods).* / *Sold ₹30,000 to Ramesh — balance nil.*

### 2.4 Money held back by a customer or contractor is Retention held, on its own account
When a customer or contractor holds back part of what is due until the work is accepted, that amount is *Retention held by – {party}*, an asset on the party's page beside the running account. It is not left in the khata, because the khata would then show money not yet due as overdue. When it falls due, an entry with no money moved (*Retention released*) moves it into the khata; when it is paid, it is money in.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-06-4): the owner's 13 Oct 2026 deposit reasoning applies — netting it in the khata makes money not yet due look overdue.
**Example.** Work billed ₹5,00,000; ₹25,000 held back: `Dr Sharma Builders A/c ₹4,75,000 · Dr Retention held by Sharma Builders A/c ₹25,000 · Cr Contract Income A/c ₹5,00,000`. On release: `Dr Sharma Builders A/c ₹25,000 · Cr Retention held by Sharma Builders A/c ₹25,000`.
Diary: *Gave on credit ₹4,75,000 to Sharma Builders; ₹25,000 is held back as Retention.* / *Retention released: ₹25,000 moved to Sharma Builders' khata — now due. No money moved.*

### 2.5 What will not come is written off by a plain entry
A debt known not to come is a loss: *Written off* (an expense head) is debited and the person's account credited. There is no provision first. The same entry serves any balance, not only a trade debt (see 8.2).
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch. 9 §9.8 [4]; ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.4 item (v) [16]. *Written off* as the head: owner ruling 12 Oct 2026; no provision, ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-06-5): khata basis.
**Example.** `Dr Written Off A/c ₹2,500 · Cr Mohan A/c ₹2,500`.
Diary: *Written off ₹2,500 — Mohan has gone bankrupt; You will get nothing.*

### 2.6 A debt written off and later paid is income, not a credit to the person
When money comes in from a person whose debt was written off, it is not credited to his account. It is credited to *Bad debts recovered*, an income head of the year in which it arrives.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.4 item (v) pp. 7.20–7.21; True/False 13 and answer pp. 7.61, 7.67 [16]; Ch. 2 Unit 6 §6.5.1 p. 2.138 [12]; NCERT Part I ch. 6 rectification illustrations [5].
**Example.** `Dr Cash A/c ₹1,200 · Cr Bad Debts Recovered A/c ₹1,200`.
Diary: *Money in ₹1,200 (Income: Bad debts recovered) — Mohan paid what I had written off last year.*

### 2.7 No provision for doubtful debts
Because the book records money when it moves and dues when they arise, it does not set aside an estimate for debts that may not be paid. A provision presupposes receivables on an accrual basis. This holds whether or not the book has opted into year-end adjustments: a provision is not among them.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.4 adjustments (6), (7), p. 7.26 [16]; NCERT Part II ch. 9 §9.9 [4]. No provision by default: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-06-7, O-01-6): khata basis.
**Example.** Customers owe ₹13,000 at the year end and one of them may not pay. No entry, in any book; if a debtor is later known not to pay, the balance is written off then.
Diary: *Nothing recorded for debts that may not be paid. Written off only when You will get nothing.*

## 3. Advances and imprest

### 3.1 Money handed over to be spent for the book is an Advance on its own account
Money given to a driver, foreman, caretaker, sewadar, member or a clerk to spend on the book's behalf, and an advance against salary, goes to *Advance – {person}*, an asset shown on the person's page beside his running account. It is not udhaar: money that must be accounted for with bills is not a debt in the ordinary sense. The rule is the same for members and for outsiders.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 p. 7.41 [16]; Ch. 9 §3.1 p. 9.8 [18] (advances carried as an asset). One account for members and outsiders ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-07-1).
**Example.** `Dr Advance – Gurdev (driver) A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹5,000`.
Diary: *Gave ₹5,000 as Advance to Gurdev for the trip.*

### 3.2 Bills clear the advance, one entry for any number of heads
When the bills come in, each expense is debited and the advance account credited, in one entry that may carry several heads.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.3 (imprest reimbursement, by analogy) [1]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-07-2).
**Example.** `Dr Fuel A/c ₹2,200 · Dr Tolls A/c ₹900 · Dr Repairs A/c ₹1,400 · Cr Advance – Gurdev A/c ₹4,500`.
Diary: *Gurdev's bills: Fuel ₹2,200, Tolls ₹900, Repairs ₹1,400 — ₹4,500 cleared from his Advance.*

### 3.3 The unspent part comes back by money in; the account rests at nil
A balance returned is cash or bank debited and the advance account credited. When nothing remains with the person, the account stands at nil. A top-up later is a fresh hand-out to the same account.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-07-3): follows from 3.1.
**Example.** `Dr Cash A/c ₹500 · Cr Advance – Gurdev A/c ₹500`.
Diary: *Money in ₹500 — Gurdev returned the unspent Advance.*

### 3.4 A remainder with no bills has three outcomes, chosen in the settlement
When bills do not cover the advance, the settlement entry chooses one of three: (1) it stays open and carried forward, still to be accounted for — the default; (2) it moves to the person's own account as money he owes (`Dr person · Cr Advance`); (3) it is written off to an expense head.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-07-4): the three are the outcomes real books show.
**Example.** Advance ₹5,000, bills ₹4,500, no bills for ₹500. Option 2: `Dr Gurdev A/c ₹500 · Cr Advance – Gurdev A/c ₹500`. Option 3: `Dr Written Off A/c ₹500 · Cr Advance – Gurdev A/c ₹500`.
Diary: *₹500 has no bill: kept open / moved to Gurdev's khata (You will get ₹500) / Written off.*

### 3.5 A salary advance is recovered in the salary entry
When salary is paid, one entry shows the full salary, the part recovered from the advance and the net paid. The advance is an asset until recovered.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 p. 7.67 (advance salary is an asset) [16]. One-entry form ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-07-5).
**Example.** `Dr Salary A/c ₹15,000 · Cr Advance – Sunil A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹12,000`.
Diary: *Paid Sunil's salary ₹15,000: ₹3,000 taken off his Advance, ₹12,000 Paid from Bank.*

### 3.6 A float for a person outside the firm is an advance, not an imprest; the word is not used
An imprest is a fixed float held by a petty cashier inside the business. A float given to anyone else (a mason, a labour contractor, a committee member) is an *Advance – {person}* (3.1), topped up as needed.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.3 [1]; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 5 §5.4 (imprest is for a petty cashier) [11]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-07-6).
**Example.** `Dr Advance – Mason Bhola A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹10,000`; top-up `Dr Advance – Mason Bhola A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹5,000`.
Diary: *Gave ₹10,000 as Advance to Bhola; later gave ₹5,000 more.*

### 3.7 Petty cash held by a clerk under an imprest: refilled only against bills
A fixed sum is handed to the petty cashier. He pays small bills from it, and when he presents bills the same amount is returned, so he again holds the full float. The cashier receives no cash other than that. The float is its own account, *Petty cash*, a kind of Cash: handing it over and refilling it are Transfers from the main cash (1.3), and the bills are debited to their heads and credited to *Petty cash* when they are presented, and in any case at each month end and at the year end.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.1.3 [1]; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 5 §5.4 pp. 2.112–2.114 [11].
**Example.** Float handed over: `Dr Petty Cash A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹5,000`. Bills ₹3,200 (conveyance ₹1,200, postage ₹800, stationery ₹1,200): `Dr Conveyance A/c ₹1,200 · Dr Postage A/c ₹800 · Dr Stationery A/c ₹1,200 · Cr Petty Cash A/c ₹3,200`. Refill: `Dr Petty Cash A/c ₹3,200 · Cr Cash A/c ₹3,200`.
Diary: *Transfer ₹5,000 from Cash in hand to Petty cash — Suresh's float.* / *Suresh's bills: Conveyance ₹1,200, Postage ₹800, Stationery ₹1,200 — ₹3,200 from Petty cash.* / *Transfer ₹3,200 from Cash in hand to Petty cash — float back to ₹5,000.*

## 4. Loans

### 4.1 A loan taken is a liability, credited to the lender
When a loan is taken, the lender's loan account is credited and the bank or cash debited. A loan due within a year is short-term; one due later is long-term.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 ("6% Loan Account") [1]; Part II ch. 8 (current and long-term liabilities) [3].
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹25,000 · Cr Loan from Dayal Traders A/c ₹25,000`.
Diary: *Money in ₹25,000 — Took a Loan from Dayal Traders (What I owe).*

### 4.2 A loan to or from a relative is udhaar unless it carries interest or a schedule
Money given to a relative and expected back sits in his running account on the debit side (*You will get*); money taken from him sits on the credit side (*You will give*). A loan that carries interest or has a repayment schedule gets its own account, *Loan to – {person}* or *Loan from – {person}*, on the person's page beside his running account.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 1 §1.7.1, p. 2.16 [26] (a person's account is a personal account). Own account for a loan with interest or a schedule: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-02-4): matches the per-person deposit ruling and stops an interest-bearing loan hiding in everyday udhaar.
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Loan from Ramesh A/c ₹50,000` (at 12% a year, to be repaid in two years).
Diary: *Money in ₹50,000 — Loan from Ramesh at 12%, to be repaid in two years.*

### 4.3 An instalment is split into principal and interest from the lender's statement
One entry with two debits: the loan (principal) and interest on loan (expense), against the bank. The figures come from the lender's statement or schedule. The loan's balance falls only by the principal.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 10 Unit 4 §4.6 pp. 10.143–10.144 [19]; Ch. 3 §5(vi) p. 3.10 [13]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-08-1).
**Example.** `Dr Loan from Punjab Bank A/c ₹7,500 · Dr Interest on Loan A/c ₹1,800 · Cr Bank A/c ₹9,300`.
Diary: *Paid ₹9,300 from Bank: ₹7,500 off the Loan, ₹1,800 interest (from the bank's statement).*

### 4.4 Repaying principal alone is the reverse of taking the loan
A repayment of principal debits the lender's loan account and credits the bank or cash.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 10 Unit 4 §4.6 pp. 10.143–10.144 [19] (the loan account is debited with the cash paid).
**Example.** `Dr Loan from Ramesh A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹10,000`.
Diary: *Paid ₹10,000 to Ramesh — part of the Loan returned (What I owe falls).*

### 4.5 A KCC or cash-credit account is a loan account used as money
A Kisan Credit Card or cash-credit limit that the bank keeps as its own account is a loan: drawing from it pays costs or moves money to the bank; interest the bank charges is debited to interest and credited to the loan; repayment debits the loan and credits the bank.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.6 p. 7.39 (cash credit is a short-term loan) [16]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-08-3): the farm and agriculture books keep it this way.
**Example.** Draw: `Dr Seed A/c ₹40,000 · Cr KCC Loan A/c ₹40,000`. Interest: `Dr Interest on KCC A/c ₹1,600 · Cr KCC Loan A/c ₹1,600`. Repay: `Dr KCC Loan A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹50,000`.
Diary: *Spent ₹40,000 on Seed — from KCC.* / *Bank charged ₹1,600 interest on KCC.* / *Transfer ₹50,000 from Bank to KCC — repaid.*

### 4.6 If the lender's statement and the book differ on interest, the lender's governs
The book records the lender's figures. A difference found later is put right by a correction entry to interest (see section 7).
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-08-2): the user cannot compute the bank's interest; the lender's ledger is the record of the loan.
**Example.** Book showed interest ₹1,800, statement shows ₹1,850: `Dr Interest on Loan A/c ₹50 · Cr Loan from Punjab Bank A/c ₹50` — note: "corrects entry no. {n} of {date}".
Diary: *Interest was ₹50 short against the bank's statement; corrected.*

### 4.7 Interest is recorded when it is charged or paid
Interest on a loan taken is an expense when the lender charges it (on the statement) or when it is paid. For a hand loan between people, it is recorded when paid or when added to the *Loan from – {person}* account by agreement (4.2). Nothing is recorded for time alone; a business book, or a trust or committee, that opts into year-end adjustments may add the interest due to date (*04 — Year end and reports*).
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch. 9 §9.3 [4] describes interest on a time basis in accrual books; ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 p. 7.23 [16]. Interest when charged or paid: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-08-5): khata basis.
**Example.** `Dr Interest on Loan A/c ₹500 · Cr Bank A/c ₹500`.
Diary: *Paid ₹500 from Bank — interest on the loan.*

### 4.8 Interest on a loan given is income when received or when charged by agreement
Interest on money lent is income when it arrives, or when it is added to the *Loan to – {person}* account by agreement (4.2). Not by time alone.
**Source:** AS 9 ¶8.2 [23] and ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 p. 7.24 [16] (accrual books). Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-08-7): khata basis.
**Example.** `Dr Cash A/c ₹1,500 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹1,500` (3 months at 12% on ₹50,000, received).
Diary: *Money in ₹1,500 (Income: Interest) — from my friend on his ₹50,000 loan.*

### 4.9 Nothing is recorded for the benefit of an interest-free loan
A loan without interest shows no notional interest, on either side.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-08-6): a notional benefit is not a transaction.
**Example.** No entry for interest.
Diary: *Nothing — the loan carries no interest.*

## 5. Assets and deposits

### 5.1 A purchase that lasts more than a year is a Thing owned; the rest is expense
Money spent for something that will be used for more than a year (vehicle, machine, land, gold, furniture) is a Thing owned, unless it is small for this book (materiality). Money spent for what is used up in the year is an expense. Small tools and consumables are expenses. There is no rupee limit: a fixed figure would be wrong for a household and a firm alike.
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch. 8 §8.2.1–8.2.3 pp. 279–281 [3]; Part I ch. 2 §2.2.12 (materiality) [7]; ICAI Framework ¶49, 93, 95, 96 [20]; ICAI BoS Ch. 1 Unit 2 p. 1.48 [8]. No limit: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-9).
**Example.** `Dr Furniture A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹12,000` (a steel cupboard); `Dr Wages A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹12,000` (a month's wages).
Diary: *Bought Furniture ₹12,000 — Thing owned.* / *Spent ₹12,000 on Wages — Expense.*

### 5.2 A Thing owned is carried at its cost, including the cost of putting it to use
Cost is the price plus freight, insurance in transit, installation, registration and commission; for land, stamp duty and registry.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 7 §7.5.1 pp. 232–233 [6]; AS 10 ¶17(b), 18, 20, 21 [24].
**Example.** `Dr Flour Mill A/c ₹55,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹55,000` (₹50,000 price + ₹5,000 carriage and fitting). Plot: `Dr Plot No. 7 A/c ₹9,80,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹9,80,000`, then `Dr Plot No. 7 A/c ₹68,600 · Cr Bank A/c ₹68,600` for stamp duty and registry; it stands at ₹10,48,600.
Diary: *Bought a flour mill ₹50,000 and paid ₹5,000 to bring and fit it — it stands at ₹55,000.*

### 5.3 Repairs are expense; an addition or improvement is added to cost
Day-to-day servicing, repairs and the replacement of a worn-out part are an expense. An addition or extension that adds to what the thing can do is added to its cost. Where a major part is replaced and its cost is added to the thing, the cost of the part taken out is removed from the thing at the same time.
**Source:** AS 10 ¶12, 13, 78 [24]; NCERT Part I ch. 7 §7.10 p. 259 and ch. 6 §6.4.4 p. 190 [6][5]; ICAI BoS Ch. 1 Unit 3 §3.2(c)–(d) p. 1.63 and Illustration 1 (5), pp. 1.64–1.65 (a worn-out part replaced is revenue expense) [9].
**Example.** `Dr Repairs A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹2,000` (tap); `Dr Shop Building A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹30,000` (a store room added at the back).
Diary: *Spent ₹2,000 on Repairs.* / *Paid ₹30,000 to add a store room — added to the shop's cost.*

### 5.4 No depreciation unless the book opts in; the report must say so
Depreciation is not charged by default. Things owned stay at cost, and every report that shows them carries the sentence: **"Things owned are shown at what they cost; no depreciation is charged."** A business book, or a trust or committee, may opt into depreciation at year end, at a rate for each thing; *04 — Year end and reports* describes the step. The sources treat the charge as the rule for a book kept on the accrual basis, and say that leaving it out overstates both the thing and the result; the book therefore states the omission rather than hiding it.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 7 §7.4.3 p. 232 [6]; AS 10 ¶50, 52 [24]; AS 1 ¶27 (departures are disclosed) [21]; ICAI BoS Ch. 5 §1.1 pp. 5.2–5.4 [15]. Omission by default: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-1): households, farms and trucks do not keep it; the books make no compliance claim.
**Example.** Computer bought ₹45,000; rate 20% a year. By default: no entry; it stays at ₹45,000. If opted in: `Dr Depreciation A/c ₹9,000 · Cr Computer A/c ₹9,000`.
Diary: *Computer: Thing owned ₹45,000. No depreciation is charged; the report says so.*

### 5.5 Land, gold and jewellery are not depreciable
An asset with no limited useful life stays at cost even in a book that opts into depreciation.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 5 p. 5.41 (land) [15]; NCERT Part I ch. 7 Box 1 p. 228 [6]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-2).
**Example.** Gold bought `Dr Gold A/c ₹3,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹3,00,000`; no depreciation entry, ever.
Diary: *Bought Gold ₹3,00,000 — Thing owned.*

### 5.6 A sale of a Thing owned shows a gain or loss against what it stood at
On sale, the thing leaves the book at its carrying amount (cost, where no depreciation is kept). The difference from the money received is a gain or a loss. A cost of selling it, such as a dealer's brokerage, is an expense of its own; it is not set against the gain.
**Source:** AS 10 ¶74, 75 [24]; NCERT Part I ch. 7 §7.9 pp. 249–251 [6]. AS 10 ¶79 measures the gain on the net disposal proceeds, after the costs of selling; this book shows the gain before them and the cost as an expense, which leaves the year's result unchanged (owner, 26 Oct 2026; a point for the bookkeeper, PLAN-209).
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹15,50,000 · Cr Plot No. 14 A/c ₹12,00,000 · Cr Profit on Sale of Plot A/c ₹3,50,000`; the dealer's brokerage paid two days later, `Dr Brokerage A/c ₹31,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹31,000`. Loss: `Dr Bank A/c ₹40,000 · Dr Loss on Sale of Scooter A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Scooter A/c ₹50,000`.
Diary: *Money in ₹15,50,000 — sold Plot No. 14, which stood at ₹12,00,000: Income ₹3,50,000.* / *Money out ₹31,000 — dealer's brokerage on selling Plot No. 14: Expense.* / *Sold the Scooter for ₹40,000, which stood at ₹50,000: Expense ₹10,000.*

### 5.7 A Thing owned bought on credit, or with a lender's money, is one entry
A Thing owned bought on credit is debited at its full cost and the seller's account credited. When a lender pays the seller (a vehicle loan), one entry debits the Thing owned in full, credits the loan with the part the lender paid and credits the bank or cash with the margin the buyer paid; the lender's part does not pass through the book's bank.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.6 item 5(ii) p. 140 [1] (credit purchase of a non-goods item, by analogy) and ch. 6 §6.4.4 p. 190 [5]. Lender's part: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-6, O-12-4): follows the truck book.
**Example.** `Dr Truck A/c ₹18,00,000 · Cr Loan from Punjab Bank A/c ₹15,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹3,00,000`. Credit purchase: `Dr Furniture A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Rao Furnishings A/c ₹8,000`.
Diary: *Bought a Truck ₹18,00,000: Loan from Punjab Bank ₹15,00,000, Paid ₹3,00,000 from Bank.* / *Took Furniture ₹8,000 on credit from Rao Furnishings (You will give).*

### 5.8 An unfinished building is a Thing owned at cost; a trust may expense it
Money spent building a hall or house is *Building under construction*, a Thing owned at cost, moved to the finished building when complete. General running costs and start-up losses are not part of it. A trust or committee may instead charge construction as an expense of the year; its statements then print the standing note in *04 — Year end and reports*, 4.3.
**Source:** AS 10 ¶7, 9, 17(b), 18, 20, 21, 82(b) [24]. The trust alternative: owner ruling 13 Oct 2026 (construction is an expense head for a trust); default ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-7).
**Example.** `Dr Hall Under Construction A/c ₹2,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹2,00,000`; the same month `Dr Office Expenses A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹6,000`.
Diary: *Paid ₹2,00,000 for materials and labour on the new Hall — Thing owned, not an expense.*

### 5.9 A fixed or recurring deposit is a Deposit, not a Thing owned
A fixed deposit, a recurring deposit or any investment placed with a bank or institution is its own type, *Deposit*: an asset at the amount placed plus interest added. It is not a Thing owned and it is not part of the cash and bank balance. A recurring deposit instalment has the same shape each time.
**Source:** Owner ruling 13 Oct 2026; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 1 p. 2.16 (a fixed deposit is an asset) [26]; AS 13 ¶3.1, 32, 34 [25] (investments, by analogy). Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-3).
**Example.** `Dr Fixed Deposit A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000`; RD instalment `Dr Recurring Deposit A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹2,000`.
Diary: *Transfer ₹1,00,000 from Bank to Deposit (fixed deposit).* / *Transfer ₹2,000 from Bank to Deposit (monthly RD).*

### 5.10 Interest on a deposit is recorded when the bank credits it
Interest is income when the bank credits it (statement or passbook). If the bank adds it to a cumulative deposit, the Deposit rises; if it is paid out, it is money in. At maturity the amount placed returns as a Transfer (a capital receipt), and the interest is income. Accrual by time applies only if a business book, or a trust or committee, opts into year-end adjustments.
**Source:** AS 9 ¶8.2 [23]; ICAI BoS Ch. 1 Unit 3 §3.4 Illustration 4(d) p. 1.67 [9]; Ch. 7 Unit 1 p. 7.19 and adjustment (2) p. 7.24 [16]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-4, O-09-5): khata basis; follows the bank's record.
**Example.** Interest added to a cumulative deposit: `Dr Fixed Deposit A/c ₹4,000 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹4,000`. Paid out: `Dr Bank A/c ₹4,000 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹4,000`. Maturity: `Dr Bank A/c ₹5,40,000 · Cr Fixed Deposit A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Interest Received A/c ₹40,000`.
Diary: *Bank added ₹4,000 interest to the Deposit (Income).* / *Deposit matured: ₹5,00,000 Deposit back, ₹40,000 Income.*

### 5.11 A security deposit held from a person is a liability on its own account
A deposit held from a tenant or other person is *Deposit held – {person}*, a liability, shown on his page beside his running account and never netted into it.
**Source:** ICAI Framework ¶49(b) [20]. Owner ruling 13 Oct 2026 (own account per person); ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-8).
**Example.** `Dr Bank A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹30,000`.
Diary: *Money in ₹30,000 — Deposit held from tenant Sharma (What I owe).*

### 5.12 A security deposit paid is an asset on its own account
A deposit given to a landlord or supplier is *Deposit paid – {person}*, an asset.
**Source:** ICAI Framework ¶49(a) [20]. Owner ruling 13 Oct 2026; ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-8).
**Example.** `Dr Deposit paid – Kapoor Properties A/c ₹60,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹60,000`.
Diary: *Money out ₹60,000 — Deposit paid to Kapoor Properties (What I have).*

### 5.13 A deposit ends by return, set-off or forfeiture
Returned: the deposit account is debited and cash or bank credited. Set off against rent or dues, when agreed: the deposit account is debited and the person's account credited; no money moves. Forfeited: the deposit account is debited and *Deposit forfeited* (income) credited. A deposit paid works the same way, mirrored.
**Source:** ICAI Framework ¶49 [20]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-09-8, O-12-2): follows the per-person deposit ruling.
**Example.** Returned: `Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹30,000`. Set off against ₹12,000 rent due: `Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Sharma A/c ₹12,000`. Forfeited: `Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Deposit Forfeited A/c ₹5,000`.
Diary: *Money out ₹30,000 — Deposit returned to Sharma.* / *₹12,000 of his Deposit adjusted against rent he owed — no money moved.* / *₹5,000 of Sharma's Deposit kept (Income).*

## 6. Goods bought for sale

### 6.1 Goods bought to sell go to one head, *Goods bought for sale*
Goods bought for resale are debited to *Goods bought for sale* when bought. Things that are not goods for sale (furniture, machinery, stationery) never go there.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 6 Fig. 6.2 p. 182 and §6.4.4 p. 190 [5]; Part II ch. 8 §8.4.4 p. 293 [3].
**Example.** `Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹75,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹75,000`.
Diary: *Spent ₹75,000 on Goods bought for sale — rice and pulses.*

### 6.2 A book that counts no stock says what its result assumes
Without a stock count, the result of trading is sales less goods bought less direct expenses, and it holds only if nothing bought is left unsold. Every report that gives such a result carries the sentence: **"No stock is counted. Everything bought for sale is treated as sold or used in the year it was bought."** If goods remain, the result is understated by their cost.
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch. 8 §8.4.4 pp. 293–294 [3]; ICAI BoS Ch. 4 §4.3 p. 4.10 [14]. Wording ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-10-1).
**Example.** Goods bought ₹75,000, sales ₹1,25,000, wages ₹8,000: result ₹1,25,000 − ₹75,000 − ₹8,000 = ₹42,000, assuming nothing is left on the shelf.
Diary: *Bought ₹75,000, sold ₹1,25,000, wages ₹8,000: ₹42,000 left over. No stock is counted.*

### 6.3 A book may enter one closing-stock figure at year end
Goods on the shelf when a book opens are debited to *Goods bought for sale* in the opening entry (*01*, opening balances of things owned). In every later year, a closing-stock figure entered at the previous year end is taken back into *Goods bought for sale* on the first day of the new year; a book that entered none starts the year with no stock.
A count is not required. A book that chooses to enter a figure for unsold goods does so once, at year end: the asset *Closing stock* is debited and *Goods bought for sale* credited, and the entry is reversed on the first day of the next year. Quantities are never tracked.
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch. 9 §9.2 pp. 320–321 [4]; ICAI BoS Ch. 4 §4.1 p. 4.8, §6 p. 4.19 [14]. Purchase adjustment chosen, and no count required: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-10-2, O-10-3). *04 — Year end and reports* describes the option.
**Example.** `Dr Closing Stock A/c ₹15,000 · Cr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹15,000`; next year's first day `Dr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹15,000 · Cr Closing Stock A/c ₹15,000`.
Diary: *Counted unsold goods on 31 March: ₹15,000 at cost — added to What I have; the year's goods bought fall by ₹15,000.*

## 7. Errors and corrections

### 7.1 A posted entry is never altered
Nothing is rubbed out, overwritten or deleted after posting. An error is corrected by a new entry. (Only before posting may a wrong figure in the first record be struck through with one line, the right figure written above and initialled.)
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 6 §6.6.2 pp. 196–197 [5]; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 6 §6.5 p. 2.131 [12]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-11-2).
**Example.** Wrong: `Dr Mohan A/c ₹1,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹1,000` for a sale of ₹10,000. The wrong entry stays; the correction is added (7.2).
Diary: *The sale to Mohan was written as ₹1,000 instead of ₹10,000. The old line stays; a new line corrects it.*

### 7.2 A correction names the entry it fixes
Every correcting entry carries a note naming the entry it fixes (its number and date). Every correction is a balanced entry: it cancels the wrong effect and restores the right one. An error in one account only cannot arise, because every entry is posted from a balanced day-book entry.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 6 §6.6.1 pp. 192–194 and Illustration 1 p. 205 (narration) [5]. Citing the entry: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-11-5): the audit trail the users need.
**Example.** `Dr Mohan A/c ₹9,000 · Cr Sales A/c ₹9,000` — note: "corrects entry no. 41 of 12 Aug: sale of ₹10,000 was entered as ₹1,000".
Diary: *Added the missing ₹9,000 to Mohan and to Sales, with a note pointing to the 12 Aug entry.*

### 7.3 Posted to the wrong account, right amount: move it
An amount put against the wrong person or head is moved by one entry. No money moves.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 6 §6.6.1 pp. 192–194 [5]; Ch. 4 §4.6 item 3 p. 140 [1].
**Example.** `Dr Rent A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Landlord A/c ₹2,000` — note: "corrects entry no. 17: rent was written against the landlord's name". Posted to Ram instead of Mohan: `Dr Mohan A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Ram A/c ₹10,000`.
Diary: *Rent of ₹2,000 was written against the landlord's khata; moved to Rent.*

### 7.4 A difference in the cash count is entered that day: to the person minding the cash, or to *Cash short / over*
The count includes cheques received and not yet banked (1.3); a count of petty cash includes the bills in hand not yet entered (3.7). Whenever the cash counted differs from the cash book, the difference is entered that day, with a note. A shortage the person minding the galla or cash box must make good goes on that person's own page, to be recovered. A small or unexplained difference goes to *Cash short / over*: an expense when short, income when over. The person counting chooses which. If the cause is found later, a correction entry moves it to the right account. If the owner admits taking the money, it is Drawings instead.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-11-1): no Indian source treats it; with daily counts a suspense balance would be an unexplained figure nobody clears. A shortage to the person minding the cash: owner ruling, 27 Oct 2026 (bookkeeper review pack C11; ADR 2026-10-27). The owner's-mixed-book case: ICAI BoS Ch. 9 §4.1 item (3) p. 9.15 [18].
**Example.** Short ₹300: `Dr Cash Short / Over A/c ₹300 · Cr Cash A/c ₹300`. Found to be a missed bill for conveyance: `Dr Conveyance A/c ₹300 · Cr Cash Short / Over A/c ₹300` — note: "corrects entry no. 52".
Diary: *Cash in hand counted ₹300 less than the book; written as Cash short.* / *Found it: Spent ₹300 on Conveyance, bill missed.*
Short ₹2,000 at the shop counter, minded by Gurpreet, who will make it good: `Dr Gurpreet A/c ₹2,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹2,000`. Diary: *Counter cash ₹2,000 short; Gurpreet was minding the galla and will make it good.*

### 7.5 No suspense balance; unsorted bank lines wait as *Not yet sorted*
Entries cannot be one-sided, so no difference arises that needs parking in a suspense account, and a suspense balance is never carried into closing statements. A bank-statement line not yet given an account waits as *Not yet sorted* and must be sorted before the year closes.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 6 §6.5.2 p. 2.138 (a suspense balance is "not real"; find the errors) [12]; NCERT Part I ch. 6 pp. 197–199, 202 [5] (suspense is temporary). No suspense: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-11-3).
**Example.** Bank credit ₹7,500 of unknown origin: shown as *Not yet sorted* money in; sorted later: `Dr Not Yet Sorted A/c ₹7,500 · Cr Mohan A/c ₹7,500`.
Diary: *Money in ₹7,500 — Not yet sorted. Later: from Mohan.*

### 7.6 A correction to a closed year is made in the current year, with a note
A closed year is never reopened. The correction is entered today, dated the day it is made, with a note naming the year it belongs to. A correction that changes an income or an expense goes to that head's line for earlier years (*Rent — earlier year*), shown as its own line in this year's statement, so this year's figures do not carry last year's item unseen.
**Source:** AS 5 ¶4.3, 15 [22] (the nature and amount of prior-period items are shown separately). NCERT Part I ch. 6 §6.6.3 p. 202 [5] and ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 6 §6.5.3 p. 2.147 [12] use a Profit and Loss Adjustment account for the same purpose; this book uses a line per head instead, ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-11-4): one rule for books with and without a profit and loss account.
**Example.** Last year's March rent of ₹3,000 was missed: `Dr Rent — Earlier Year A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Landlord A/c ₹3,000` — note: "belongs to year ended 31 Mar 2026; found 14 May 2026".
Diary: *Found in May that March's rent ₹3,000 was missed; written now as Rent — earlier year, with a note that it belongs to last year.*

### 7.7 What a balanced trial balance does not show
Totals that agree prove only that every debit has a matching credit and that posting and adding are right. They do not show: a transaction omitted completely; a right amount put to the wrong account; an item of the wrong kind (capital as expense); or two errors that cancel.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 6 §6.2.1 pp. 182–183, §6.4.1–6.4.5 pp. 189–191 [5].
**Example.** `Dr Repairs A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹30,000` for a store room added to the shop; the totals agree, yet the shop building is understated by ₹30,000. Corrected: `Dr Shop Building A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Repairs A/c ₹30,000`.
Diary: *Paid ₹30,000 to add a store room but wrote Repairs; moved to Shop Building.*

### 7.8 When two sides differ, look for half and for nines
If a difference between two totals divides by 2, an item of half that amount may be on the wrong side. If it divides by 9, two digits may be swapped. Also re-add, compare against the statement, compare with last year.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 6 §6.5 p. 191 [5].
**Example.** ₹750 received from Karim was entered as money out instead of money in, so cash is ₹1,500 too low (twice ₹750). One balanced entry cancels the wrong effect and restores the right one: `Dr Cash A/c ₹1,500 · Cr Karim A/c ₹1,500` — note: "corrects entry no. 33 of 9 Jun".
Diary: *The two totals were ₹1,500 apart, so I looked for a ₹750 item on the wrong side — Karim's — and corrected it.*

## 8. Entries that move no money

### 8.1 What they are for
An entry with no cash or bank in it is a *journal entry*: a transfer between heads, a write-off, a settlement against a deposit or advance, a correction, an adjustment, an item taken in kind. Each carries a short note saying why.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.6 pp. 139–140 [1]; ICAI BoS Ch. 2 Unit 4 §4.7 pp. 2.96–2.97 [10]. Notes: NCERT Part I ch. 7 p. 264 and ch. 6 Illustration 1 p. 205 [6][5].
**Example.** `Dr Written Off A/c ₹2,500 · Cr Mohan A/c ₹2,500` — note: "Mohan insolvent".
Diary: *Written off Mohan's ₹2,500 — he is insolvent. No money moved.*

### 8.2 Any balance known not to come back may be written off
Rent due, an advance, a loan to a relative: any balance known not to return is written off by debiting *Written off* or a named head such as *Rent written off*, and crediting the account.
**Source:** NCERT Part II ch. 9 §9.8 [4] (bad debts), extended; owner ruling 12 Oct 2026 (*Written off* head); ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-12-1).
**Example.** `Dr Rent Written Off A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Sharma A/c ₹6,000` (tenant left owing rent).
Diary: *Written off ₹6,000 rent — tenant Sharma left; You will get nothing.*

### 8.3 A deposit held set off against rent due: no money moves
When agreed, the deposit is used against what the person owes.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-12-2): follows the per-person deposit rules (5.13).
**Example.** `Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Sharma A/c ₹12,000`.
Diary: *₹12,000 of Sharma's Deposit adjusted against his rent — no money moved.*

### 8.4 An advance settled against bills with some part unsupported
One entry: each head with a bill is debited; an unsupported part is either debited to *Written off* (accepted as spent) or to the person's account (money he owes); the advance account is credited with the whole.
**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-12-3): follows 3.2 and 3.4.
**Example.** `Dr Fuel A/c ₹2,200 · Dr Tolls A/c ₹900 · Dr Written Off A/c ₹300 · Dr Gurdev A/c ₹100 · Cr Advance – Gurdev A/c ₹3,500`.
Diary: *Gurdev's advance ₹3,500: bills Fuel ₹2,200, Tolls ₹900; ₹300 Written off; ₹100 he owes (You will get).*

### 8.5 A business cost paid from the owner's or a partner's own pocket is Own money brought in
When the owner meets a business cost from private funds, the asset or expense is debited and the owner's capital credited. For a partnership with fixed capital the credit goes to the partner's current account (see *03 — Entities*).
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 9 §4.1 item (2) p. 9.15, §4.5 pp. 9.22–9.23, §3.1 p. 9.6 [18]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-12-5).
**Example.** `Dr Rent A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Ramesh Capital A/c ₹3,000`.
Diary: *Spent ₹3,000 on Rent — Own money, paid by Ramesh from his pocket.*

### 8.6 Goods taken by the owner for the house are Drawings
Goods used other than for sale are taken out of *Goods bought for sale*, and the suitable account is debited — for the owner's own use, Drawings.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.5 item 3, pp. 7.31, 7.58 [16]. Ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-12-6).
**Example.** `Dr Drawings A/c ₹4,000 · Cr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹4,000`.
Diary: *Drawings ₹4,000 — goods taken home from the shop.*

### 8.7 Goods lost by fire or theft: the loss, the insurer's share, the rest
The loss is first taken to *Loss by fire*, credited against *Goods bought for sale*. If the insurer admits a claim, it is an asset (*Insurance claim*) until the money arrives; the part not met stays as the loss.
**Source:** ICAI BoS Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.5 item 1, pp. 7.30, 7.58 [16]. The BoS charges the uninsured part to the profit and loss account; the uninsured part stays in *Loss by fire*, an expense head, and reaches the profit and loss through the year close (ruled 20 Oct 2026, O-12-6).
**Example.** `Dr Loss by Fire A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Goods Bought for Sale A/c ₹6,000`; claim admitted ₹4,500: `Dr Insurance Claim A/c ₹4,500 · Cr Loss by Fire A/c ₹4,500`. ₹1,500 remains in Loss by fire.
Diary: *Fire destroyed ₹6,000 of goods. The insurer accepts ₹4,500 (What I have); ₹1,500 is my loss.*

### 8.8 Year-end adjustments are entries too, and only when the book opts in
Depreciation, prepaid and outstanding items, accrued income, income received in advance and a closing-stock figure are journal entries with no cash. By default none is made; only a business book, or a trust or committee, may opt in. *04 — Year end and reports* describes each option.
**Source:** NCERT Part I ch. 4 §4.6 item 2 p. 139; ch. 7 §7.1.2 p. 229 [1][6]; Part II ch. 9 §9.2–9.8 pp. 320–329 [4]. Opt-in only: ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-01-1, O-19-4): khata basis.
**Example.** In an opted-in book: `Dr Depreciation A/c ₹1,500 · Cr Furniture A/c ₹1,500`.
Diary: *Year-end: ₹1,500 of wear written off Furniture (only if this book keeps depreciation).*
