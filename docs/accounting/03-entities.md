# 03 — Entities: who keeps the book

**Status:** Draft, 20 Oct 2026 — for a bookkeeper's review.

This document says how each kind of owner keeps a book: a household, a sole proprietor, a partnership, a joint family, two books that deal with each other, a trust or committee, and a landlord. For each it fixes whose money the book holds, where the owner's own money sits, how the year's result reaches that account, and the entries that are particular to that owner. It rests on the khata basis described in *01 — Accounting foundations* (money is recorded when it moves; a due with a named person is recorded when it arises; nothing is recorded for the passage of time alone unless the book opts into year-end adjustments). Accounts, running accounts, deposits and corrections are in *02 — Accounts and transactions*; the year-end steps and the printed reports are in *04 — Year end and reports*. This document describes bookkeeping practice only; it does not claim that a book kept this way meets any Accounting Standard or statutory format.

| Entity | Whose money the book holds | Its own-money account | Its year statement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household | The household's own money | Own money | Income & Expenses; result *Saving* (or *Spent more than came in*) |
| Sole proprietor (business book) | The business's money, with the owner's stake shown separately | Capital (*Own money*), with a Drawings account | Profit & Loss |
| Partnership | The firm's money | Partners' capital (one per partner; with a Partner's current where capital is fixed) | Profit & Loss, then Profit & Loss Appropriation Account |
| Joint family | The family's money, and what each sub-family has given or taken | Family's own money | Income & Expenses; result *Saving* |
| Trust or committee | The trust's or committee's money, in funds | General Fund (and Corpus where founders gave it) | Income & Expenditure, with Receipts & Payments and the position (Balance Sheet) |
| Landlord | The owner's money, with one account per tenant | The owner's Own money | Profit & Loss (business book); Income & Expenses, result *Saving* (household) |

Plain words used throughout: *Money in, Money out, Received, Spent, You will get, You will give, Own money, Drawings / Taken for home, Partner, Partner's capital, Partner's current, Sub-family, Joint family, Fund, General Fund, Income, Expense, Saving, Spent more than came in, What I have, What I owe.* The true ledger side follows in brackets where it helps.

## 1. Sole proprietor and household

A sole proprietor keeps the business as one book and himself as another. The business book holds the business's money; it records the owner's dealings only where money moves between the business and its owner. The owner's own life stays in his own (household) book. A household book has no profit: its result is a saving.

### 1.1 The owner and the business are two books

The business is kept apart from its owner. Money the owner puts in is something the business owes back to him; money he takes out for home reduces that. The business book records the owner's personal dealings only when business money moves in or out.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 2 *Theory Base of Accounting*, §2.2.1 *Business Entity Concept* (file keac102, pp. 3–4).

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Capital A/c ₹5,00,000.
Diary: "Ramesh started his shop with ₹5,00,000 of his own cash." (*Money in* to the shop; recorded as Own money.)

### 1.2 Money the owner puts in is Own money, not income

Fresh money brought in during the year is recorded like the opening money: the account that receives it is debited and Own money (Capital) is credited. It is not income of the shop, and it never enters the year's profit. Money lent by a relative is a different thing, a loan, and is recorded in its own account (see *02 — Accounts and transactions*).

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 3, Illustration 1 (keac103 pp. 57–58) and the journal of Rohit (p. 63); NCERT Class 11 Part II, ch. 8 §8.2.2 *Receipts* (keac201 p. 5, a capital receipt is an obligation to the owner); NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 1 §1.5.10 *Profit* ("the excess of revenues of a period over its related expenses").

**Example.**
Dr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Capital A/c ₹1,00,000 (not Cr Sales or Income).
Diary: "Ramesh put another ₹1,00,000 of his own savings into the shop account. That is his money going in, not what the shop earned."

### 1.3 What the owner takes for home goes to a Drawings account

Cash or bank money taken for personal use is debited to a separate Drawings account (*Taken for home*), never to an expense. Drawings reduce the owner's own money; they are not a cost of the business. The book keeps Drawings as its own account through the year and closes it to Own money at year end.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 3, Illustration 2, entry of 29 April (keac103 p. 20) and ch. 4 §4.6 item 4 *Transfer entries* (keac104 p. 42); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.4 item (i), p. 7.19 (a separate Drawings account closed to capital; balance sheet shows capital plus profit less drawings, p. 7.41). Ruling O-13-1 settles the choice between a Drawings account and debiting capital directly in favour of the Drawings account.

**Example.**
Dr Drawings A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹5,000.
Diary: "Ramesh took ₹5,000 from the till for home expenses." (*Money out*, *Taken for home*.)

### 1.4 Goods taken for home are taken at cost

Goods the owner takes from the shop for his house are recorded as Drawings at what they cost the business, against *Goods bought for sale*. Nothing is added for the profit the shop would have made.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 4 §4.6 item 5(iii) (keac104 p. 42) and ch. 6 Illustration 7(i) (keac106 p. 34); NCERT Class 11 Part II, ch. 9, adjustment 7 (goods stated at cost); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.5 item 3, p. 7.31 (Dr Drawings, Cr Purchases; the book's account is *Goods bought for sale*). The sources give the cost-valued examples and no stated rule; the rule is ruled (O-13-3) on those examples.

**Example.**
Dr Drawings A/c ₹800 · Cr Goods bought for sale A/c ₹800.
Diary: "Ramesh took ₹800 worth of goods, at what they cost him, from the shop for his house."

### 1.5 One Own-money account and one Drawings account

A sole proprietor has one Capital (*Own money*) account and one Drawings account. He does not keep a separate current account beside Capital.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 2 §2.2.1 and ch. 4 §4.6 (one capital account); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 7 Unit 1 p. 7.19. The ICAI exposure draft's notes show an Owners' Current Account beside capital (ED-NCE Note 3b); the sources differ and the simpler form is ruled (O-13-2): it gives the same result.

**Example.**
At 31 March: Capital A/c opening ₹4,00,000, plus put in ₹1,00,000, plus the year's profit ₹80,000, less Drawings ₹60,000 = ₹5,20,000.
Diary: "My own money in the shop at year end: what I started with, what I added, what the year earned, less what I took for home — ₹5,20,000."

### 1.6 A sole proprietor allows himself no salary and no interest

What the owner takes is Drawings. He does not charge the business a salary or interest on his own money. For one owner such an entry only moves profit into his own capital and changes nothing.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-13-6): NCERT Class 11 Part II §9.12 treats interest on capital as optional and credited straight back to capital, and no source treats a salary to a sole proprietor; with one owner the entries cancel, so the book leaves them out.

**Example.**
Dr Drawings A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹20,000 (monthly home money for the year, as drawings; no Salary A/c).
Diary: "Took ₹20,000 for home this month. Not called salary."

### 1.7 At year end the result and Drawings close to Own money

The book posts the closing entries itself: income and expense heads to Profit & Loss; the profit (or loss) to Own money; Drawings to Own money. The balance sheet then shows Own money as opening, plus put in, plus profit, less drawings. The steps and the printed statements are in *04 — Year end and reports*.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part II, ch. 8 §8.6.2 items (7)–(8) (keac201 p. 26) and ch. 9 solution ("Net profit transferred to capital account"; Capital 60,000, add net profit 20,194, less drawings 6,000, keac202 pp. 44–45); NCERT Class 11 Part I ch. 4 §4.6 item 4; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 7 Unit 1 pp. 7.9–7.23 (closing entries as journals).

**Example.**
Dr Profit & Loss A/c ₹80,000 · Cr Capital A/c ₹80,000, then Dr Capital A/c ₹6,800 · Cr Drawings A/c ₹6,800 (₹6,000 cash and ₹800 goods).
Diary: "The year's profit, ₹80,000, is added to my own money. The ₹6,800 I took for home comes off it."

### 1.8 Business costs the owner pays from his own pocket are money put in

When the owner pays a business bill from his own pocket, the business debits the expense (or the supplier) and credits Own money. It is money put in, not a loan, unless he and the business agree it is a loan.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 9 §4.1 item (2), p. 9.15; §4.5, pp. 9.22–9.23 (payments to creditors out of personal cash are fresh capital brought in). Ruled as O-12-5 (source check, accepted).

**Example.**
Dr Electricity A/c ₹3,200 · Cr Capital A/c ₹3,200.
Diary: "Paid the shop's ₹3,200 electricity bill from my own pocket; it counts as money I put in."

### 1.9 The owner's own book holds his business as one account

The owner's personal book holds the business as a single account, *{Business} A/c*, at the money he put in. Money the business sends home is **income** in his personal book (*Income from {business}*), and Drawings in the business's book. Money put in stays at what was put in (its cost). The business's profit is worked out in the business's own book; the personal book takes only what comes home.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (joint family: money a family business sends home is income; money put in stays at cost), extended to every owner by ruling O-13-4. The textbooks describe only the business's side (NCERT Class 11 Part I ch. 2 §2.2.1 keeps the owner's personal dealings out of the business book); none describes the owner's own book.

**Example.**
Owner's book, money put in: Dr Ramesh Textile A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000. Money sent home: Dr Cash A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Income from Ramesh Textile A/c ₹20,000. Business's book: Dr Drawings A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹20,000.
Diary: "I put ₹1,00,000 into the shop. The shop sent ₹20,000 home this month: that is my income from the shop." (The shop's *Money out* is its Drawings.)

### 1.10 A household's statement is Income & Expenses; its result is Saving

A household's year statement is *Income & Expenses*, and its result is a *Saving* (income more than expenses) or *Spent more than came in*. Income includes salary, interest, rent, *Income from {business}* and gifts received. It never includes loans taken, money returned to the household, transfers between the household's own accounts, or a deposit coming back. A household has no profit; it does have *Own money* (its capital account), which grows by the saving.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-13-5): no source read teaches a household's accounts; NCERT Class 11 Part I §1.5.10 (profit is revenue less expenses) serves only as the test that loans, capital and returned money are not income. The words are the household's own.

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Salary A/c ₹30,000; Dr Groceries A/c ₹4,500 · Cr Cash A/c ₹4,500; at year end Dr Income & Expenses A/c ₹2,40,000 · Cr Own money A/c ₹2,40,000 (income ₹9,60,000 less expenses ₹7,20,000).
Diary: "Salary ₹30,000 came in. Groceries ₹4,500 went out. For the year ₹9,60,000 came in as income and ₹7,20,000 was spent: Saving ₹2,40,000."

## 2. Partnership

A firm's book is the firm's own. Each partner has his own capital (and, with fixed capital, a current) account. The year's profit is not simply computed and shown: it is posted through a Profit & Loss Appropriation Account into the partners' accounts. The rules below describe what the textbooks teach when the partners have made no agreement about a point, and what the book does when they have.

### 2.1 When there is no agreement, the textbook defaults apply

Where the partners have agreed nothing, the book keeps to five defaults. Profits and losses are shared equally, whatever each partner's capital. No interest is allowed on capital. No interest is charged on drawings. A partner's loan to the firm earns 6% a year. No partner has a salary for taking part in the business. Where the partners have agreed something different, the book follows the agreement.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.2.1 items (a)–(e), p. 4 (repeated in the chapter Summary, item 3, p. 35); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.5, pp. 10.11–10.12.

**Example.**
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹90,000 · Cr Ramesh Capital A/c ₹45,000 · Cr Suresh Capital A/c ₹45,000.
Diary: "No agreement. The year's profit of ₹90,000 is split half and half between Ramesh and Suresh, though Ramesh put in ₹5,00,000 and Suresh ₹1,00,000."

### 2.2 One account per partner, unless the partners agree to fix the capital

When nothing is said, each partner has one capital account that takes everything: what he puts in, his drawings, his share of profit, interest and salary. Its balance moves and can turn against him. If the partners agree, capital is fixed: the Capital account moves only for fresh capital or an agreed withdrawal of capital, and every other item goes to a separate *Partner's current* account, which may be in the partner's favour or against him.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.4(b) ("in the absence of any instruction", fluctuating), §1.4.1, Fig. 1.1 and 1.2, pp. 5–7; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.9, pp. 10.19–10.20. Which applies when the deed is silent is ruled (O-14-4) on NCERT §1.4(b).

**Example.**
Dr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Ramesh Capital A/c ₹1,00,000 (fresh capital); Dr Ramesh Current A/c ₹40,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹40,000 (drawings, fixed capital).
Diary: "Ramesh added ₹1,00,000 to his capital. He took ₹40,000 for home; with fixed capital that goes in his current account, which can run in his favour or against him."

### 2.3 Partners' opening capitals balance the firm's opening books

When the firm's books open, the partners' agreed capitals must equal what the firm has less what it owes. There is no balancing line. If the figures the partners agree do not equal it, the partners settle the difference among themselves (in their profit-sharing ratio, unless they agree otherwise) before the book opens.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.4 (all partner money goes through the capital accounts); NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 3 (assets = liabilities + capital); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 9 (capital = assets less liabilities). Ruled O-14-8 and O-04-5.

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹2,00,000 · Dr Shop Fittings A/c ₹6,00,000 · Cr Creditors A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Ramesh Capital A/c ₹4,50,000 · Cr Suresh Capital A/c ₹2,50,000.
Diary: "The firm starts with ₹2,00,000 cash and ₹6,00,000 of shop fittings, owing ₹1,00,000. What is left, ₹7,00,000, is the partners' own money: Ramesh ₹4,50,000, Suresh ₹2,50,000."

### 2.4 A partner's drawings go to his own account

Money or goods a partner takes for home is debited to his own account (capital if fluctuating, current if fixed); it is not an expense of the firm.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.4, Fig. 1.1 and 1.2 (Drawings on the debit side of the current or capital account); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.7, p. 10.14 (capital debited, or a separate drawings or current account).

**Example.**
Dr Suresh Current A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹30,000.
Diary: "Suresh took ₹30,000 for home. Charged to his own account."

### 2.5 Profit goes through the Appropriation Account and is posted

At year end the book posts the firm's net profit to the credit of the Profit & Loss Appropriation Account, enters in it everything the partners are allowed or charged, and credits what is left to each partner's account by his share. The entries are posted by the book at year close; the partner never types them. The Appropriation Account is an account of the ledger, an extension of Profit & Loss, not a table computed on the side.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.5 and §1.5.1, journal entries 1–6, Fig. 1.1–1.3, pp. 8–9; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.8, pp. 10.15–10.17. Ruled O-14-1 (accepted).

**Example.** Ramesh and Suresh share 3 : 2; fixed capitals ₹5,00,000 and ₹3,00,000; interest on capital agreed at 6%; salary to Suresh ₹60,000 agreed; drawings ₹40,000 and ₹30,000, with interest on drawings agreed (₹1,000 and ₹750); net profit ₹3,00,000. Interest on capital is ₹30,000 and ₹18,000. Divisible profit: 3,00,000 + 1,750 − 48,000 − 60,000 = ₹1,93,750, split ₹1,16,250 and ₹77,500.
Dr Profit & Loss A/c ₹3,00,000 · Cr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹3,00,000.
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹48,000 · Cr Ramesh Current A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Suresh Current A/c ₹18,000 (interest on capital).
Dr Ramesh Current A/c ₹1,000 · Dr Suresh Current A/c ₹750 · Cr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹1,750 (interest on drawings).
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹60,000 · Cr Suresh Current A/c ₹60,000 (salary).
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹1,93,750 · Cr Ramesh Current A/c ₹1,16,250 · Cr Suresh Current A/c ₹77,500 (share of profit).
Diary: "The year's profit was ₹3,00,000. Each partner gets 6% on his capital, ₹30,000 and ₹18,000. Suresh, who runs the shop, gets ₹60,000 salary. Each is charged for what he took out, ₹1,000 and ₹750. What is left, ₹1,93,750, is split 3 to 2: Ramesh ₹1,16,250, Suresh ₹77,500." Check: 48,000 + 60,000 + 1,93,750 − 1,750 = ₹3,00,000. (The textbook posts interest and salary through an expense account first and then to the Appropriation Account; the result in the partners' accounts is the same.)

### 2.6 Salary, commission and interest on capital are shares of profit, not costs

Partners' salary, commission and interest on capital are appropriations in the Profit & Loss Appropriation Account, below the profit line. They are allowed only if the partners agreed them. Interest on capital is **optional**: it is allowed only where the partners agreed it, and then at the agreed rate, on the opening capital for the year, on any addition from the date it came in, and on the reduced capital from the date of any agreed withdrawal of capital.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.5.1 and §1.5.2, pp. 8–9, 15–16 (§1.5.2 item (iii), p. 16: after a withdrawal, interest runs on the reduced capital), Illustration 5, p. 17; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.8, pp. 10.15–10.17 (both agree). The ICAI exposure draft instead shows partners' remuneration and interest on capital as charges above the profit line (ED-NCE Part II, line X; General Instructions item 4); the two sources differ and the textbook form is ruled (O-14-2). Owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (interest on capital only if agreed).

**Example.** Ramesh's capital was ₹5,00,000, and ₹1,00,000 more came in on 1 October; agreed 6% a year: 5,00,000 × 6% + 1,00,000 × 6% × 6/12 = ₹33,000.
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹33,000 · Cr Ramesh Current A/c ₹33,000.
Diary: "Ramesh gets 6% on ₹5,00,000 for the year and 6% on the extra ₹1,00,000 for the half-year it was in: ₹33,000. It comes out of the profit before the split."

### 2.7 Interest and salary are paid only out of profit

Interest on capital (where agreed) and a partner's salary are allowed only out of profit. If the profit is less than their total, the profit is shared among those entitled in proportion to the amounts due. If the firm makes a loss, none is allowed.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.9, p. 10.21 (interest on capital: if profit is less than the interest, the profit is shared in the ratio of capital); NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.5.1 note (nothing is allowed in a loss); owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026. The BoS does not speak of salary in this case; carrying the same proportion to salary is ruled (O-14-3).

**Example.** Capitals ₹1,00,000 (Ramesh) and ₹60,000 (Suresh); agreed interest 10% would be ₹10,000 and ₹6,000 = ₹16,000, but the profit is ₹8,000, shared 5 : 3.
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Ramesh Current A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Suresh Current A/c ₹3,000.
Diary: "The partners agreed 10% on capital, which needs ₹16,000. The year made only ₹8,000, so it is split in proportion: ₹5,000 and ₹3,000. Nothing is left to share."

### 2.8 Interest on a partner's loan is a cost of the firm

A partner may lend the firm money apart from his capital. It goes in its own account, *Loan from {partner}*, and carries interest at the agreed rate (6% a year if nothing is agreed). That interest is an expense charged before the profit is divided, not an appropriation of profit.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.2.1 item (d), p. 4 (6%); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.8, Illustration 2, pp. 10.16–10.17 (interest on a partner's loan "is not an appropriation of profit"). Ruled O-14-2.

**Example.** A lent ₹20,000 on 1 October (year ending 31 March); capitals ₹40,000 and ₹10,000; profit before the loan interest ₹50,600; 6% for six months is ₹600; the rest, ₹50,000, is shared equally.
Dr Interest on Loan from A A/c ₹600 · Cr Loan from A A/c ₹600 (an expense, before profit).
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹50,000 · Cr A Capital A/c ₹25,000 · Cr B Capital A/c ₹25,000.
Diary: "A lent the firm ₹20,000 for half a year. With nothing agreed he gets 6%, ₹600, taken off before the split. The remaining ₹50,000 is shared half and half."

### 2.9 A firm bill paid from a partner's pocket is money he put in

When a partner pays a firm bill from his own pocket, the firm debits the expense or the supplier and credits his capital account (fluctuating) or his current account (fixed), as money he brought in. It is a loan only if the partners agree it is; then it goes to *Loan from {partner}* as in the rule above.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 9 §4.5, pp. 9.22–9.23 (payments to creditors out of personal cash are fresh capital brought in); Ch. 10 Unit 1, p. 10.31 (a loan is separate from capital). Ruled O-14-5 and O-12-5.

**Example.**
Dr Kapoor Traders A/c ₹4,000 · Cr Ramesh Capital A/c ₹4,000.
Diary: "Ramesh paid the firm's ₹4,000 bill to Kapoor Traders from his own pocket; it counts as money he put in."

### 2.10 A change of ratio in the year is divided by time

When partners change their sharing ratio during the year, the year's profit is divided by days: the part before the change in the old ratio, the part after in the new. If the partners close the books at the change date, each part's actual profit is used instead. The value the gaining partner owes the losing partner for the change itself is settled between their capital accounts (their current accounts where capital is fixed, 2.2), in the proportion each gained or gave up; no goodwill account is opened in the firm's books.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 5 §5.3, pp. 10.173–10.174 (time basis, taught for the death of a partner; applied here by analogy; ruled O-14-6); Ch. 10 Unit 2 §2.1 pp. 10.43–10.44 and §2.6 pp. 10.61–10.62 (change of ratio settled through capital accounts; "in no case the goodwill account is to be raised"); NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 2 §2.9, Illustration 29. No source divides one year's profit between two ratios.

**Example.** Profit ₹1,20,000; 3 : 2 for the first half and equal for the second (shown at half-year for a round figure; in practice by days). First half ₹60,000 gives ₹36,000 and ₹24,000; second half gives ₹30,000 each.
Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹1,20,000 · Cr Ramesh Current A/c ₹66,000 · Cr Suresh Current A/c ₹54,000.
Settling the change itself (BoS figures): A, B, C equal partners move to 4 : 3 : 2; goodwill ₹90,000; A gains one-ninth, C loses one-ninth: Dr A's Capital A/c ₹10,000 · Cr C's Capital A/c ₹10,000 (capitals fluctuating; with fixed capitals, Dr A's Current A/c · Cr C's Current A/c).
Diary: "From 1 October Ramesh and Suresh share equally instead of 3 to 2. Ramesh got his 3-to-2 share of the first six months and half of the rest: ₹66,000; Suresh ₹54,000."

### 2.11 A partner who does not work is treated like any partner

A partner who puts money in but does not work, or whose drawings another partner enters, has the same accounts as any partner. Whoever keeps the book records the entry, with a note of who asked for it. A partner who does not work has no salary unless the partners agreed one.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.8, p. 10.15 (a partner who does not work as much as another still shares profit as agreed, or equally), and the answer to true-or-false question 6, p. 10.36 ("Even if a partner does not take part in the business he is entitled for his share of profit"); NCERT Class 12 Part I ch. 1 §1.2.1 item (e) (no salary unless agreed). Who enters a drawing for another partner is not covered by the sources; ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-14-7): the books record transactions, not who was present.

**Example.**
Dr Suresh Current A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹10,000 (entered by Ramesh; note: "taken by Suresh's son, on Suresh's phone call").
Diary: "Suresh was away; his son took ₹10,000 for him. Ramesh wrote it in Suresh's account and noted who asked."

### 2.12 What is particular to the partnership's year close

The book posts the closing entries itself: income and expense heads to Profit & Loss; the profit to the Profit & Loss Appropriation Account; and from there to the partners' capital or current accounts as above. Drawings stand in each partner's own account and need no separate closing. The partners' accounts, not one Own money line, then carry the firm's equity forward. See *04 — Year end and reports* for the printed statements.

**Source:** NCERT Class 12 Part I, ch. 1 §1.5.1, Fig. 1.3; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 10 Unit 1 §1.8. Ruled O-19-1.

**Example.**
Dr Profit & Loss A/c ₹3,00,000 · Cr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹3,00,000, then Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c ₹3,00,000 · Cr the partners' accounts as in the example of the Appropriation Account.
Diary: "At year end the firm's profit is shared out into each partner's account, and each opens the next year with his balance."

## 3. Joint family

A joint family keeps its own book, the family's. Each sub-family (a household inside the family) has one running account in it. The family may own a business, kept in its own separate book. The textbooks and ICAI pronouncements say nothing about a joint family, so the rules below are the owner's rulings, with the head-office and branch pair of accounts as the nearest textbook form. The family's book is on the khata basis.

### 3.1 The karta keeps the family's book; his own household is a sub-family

The karta is the family's manager in the books. He keeps the family's book or authorises its entries. His own household is a sub-family with its own running account, like the others; he has no separate account as karta.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-15-6): no source treats a karta; the rule follows the running-account model below. The bookkeeper's review covers it.

**Example.**
Dr Karta's household A/c ₹15,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹15,000.
Diary: "The karta took ₹15,000 for his own household's expenses. It goes in his household's account like anyone else's."

### 3.2 One running account per sub-family; it is not a debt

The family's book holds one running account per sub-family, *{Sub-family} A/c*. On the debit side: household money given, money taken that is to be returned, and bills the family pays for that household. On the credit side: what the household puts in — into the family, straight into a family business, or by paying a family-business bill from its own money. It is not a debt; it sits under Own money, in both books. The sub-family's own book holds the matching account named after the family.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026; ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §6, pp. 15.41–15.44 (converse running accounts of head office and branch), by analogy. Ruled O-15-1.

**Example.**
Family's book, money given to the household: Dr Rajinder's household A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹10,000. Rajinder's household book: Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Family A/c ₹10,000.
Diary: "The family gave Rajinder's household ₹10,000 for the month. In the family's book Rajinder's account is ₹10,000 against him; in his own book the family's account is ₹10,000 in the family's favour."

### 3.3 A sub-family's money put into a family business is a credit to its running account

When a sub-family puts its own money straight into a business the family owns, or pays the business's bill from its own pocket, the family's book credits the sub-family's running account and debits the business's account (an entry that moves no money in the family's book). The business's book credits the family's Own money, as money put in. Three books, one transaction.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (what a household puts in, straight into a family business or by paying its bill, is a credit to its account). Ruled O-15-2.

**Example.**
Family's book: Dr Sharma Textile A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Rajinder's household A/c ₹50,000. Sharma Textile's book: Dr Bank A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Family's own money A/c ₹50,000. Rajinder's household book: Dr Family A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹50,000.
Diary: "Rajinder's household put ₹50,000 into the family's textile shop. The shop has it as the family's money; Rajinder's account in the family's book is ₹50,000 in his favour."

### 3.4 Money a family business sends home is the family's income

Money a family business sends home is the family's income, *Income from {business}*. Money the family put into the business stays at what it put in. Only what comes home is income; the business's profit is worked out in the business's own book and shown in the family summary, not taken into the family's income, because that would count the same money twice.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026. Ruled O-15-3 and O-15-4. The textbooks teach money drawn from a business as drawings, a reduction of capital, for a sole proprietor and partners only (NCERT Class 11 Part I ch. 1 §1.5.16, ch. 2 §2.2.1; ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 7 Unit 1 §1.4 item (i), p. 7.19, and Ch. 9 §4.4, p. 9.22); none treats a family.

**Example.**
Family's book: Dr Cash A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Income from Sharma Textile A/c ₹20,000. Sharma Textile's book: Dr Drawings (Family) A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹20,000.
Diary: "The textile shop sent ₹20,000 home. For the family that is income from the shop; the family's ₹50,000 in the shop stays ₹50,000."

### 3.5 At year end a sub-family's account is carried forward or settled, as the family decides

At year end each sub-family's running account is either **carried forward** as it stands (most families do) or **settled**. It is settled either by the family bearing it (charged to a family expense head) or from the sub-family's share of the family's business income. A share of business income is not a reduction of the family's income: the income came home and stays income; the family **shares it out** of its own money, as a partnership shares its profit, through a *Business income shared out* account that closes to the family's own money with the year's result. In the sub-family's own book the same amount is income, *Share of family business income*. A balance carried forward opens the next year unchanged.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026; ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §7.1, p. 15.47 (the reconciled balance is carried forward, "To Balance b/d"). Ruled O-15-5. The two settling entries below are written from the ruling; no source gives them.

**Example.** Rajinder's account stands at ₹30,000 against him (more taken than put in).
Carry forward: no entry; Rajinder's household A/c ₹30,000 Dr opens the next year.
Settled by the family bearing it: Dr Household expenses A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Rajinder's household A/c ₹30,000.
Settled from his share of the business income — in the family's book: Dr Business income shared out A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Rajinder's household A/c ₹30,000; in Rajinder's household book: Dr Joint family A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Share of family business income A/c ₹30,000.
Diary: "Rajinder's household took ₹30,000 more than it put in. The family carries it to next year, or the family takes it as its own household cost, or it is set against Rajinder's share of what the shop sent home."

### 3.6 What is particular to the family's year close

The book posts the closing entries itself: income and expense heads to the result; the result (the family's Saving) to the family's own money. Sub-family accounts are not closed to it; they carry their balances forward unless the family settles them. See *04 — Year end and reports*.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026; ruled O-19-1 (a joint family's surplus to the family's own money) and O-01-3 (khata basis).

**Example.**
Dr Income & Expenses A/c ₹1,80,000 · Cr Family's own money A/c ₹1,80,000 (income ₹8,00,000 less expenses ₹6,20,000).
Diary: "The family's year: ₹8,00,000 came in as income, ₹6,20,000 was spent. Saving ₹1,80,000, added to the family's own money."

## 4. Accounts between related books

Whenever two books deal with each other — a family and a sub-family, a family and its business, an owner and his own business, a partnership and a partner's household — each keeps an account for the other. The nearest teaching is the head-office and branch pair. The rules below apply that pair, with the owner's naming rule.

### 4.1 Related books keep converse accounts, each named after the other book

Each book holds one account named after the other (*Rajinder's household A/c*, *Family A/c*, *Sharma Textile A/c*), never *Due to* or *Due from*.

A **running-account pair** (a family and a sub-family; two people) is converse balance for balance: where one is debit the other is credit, for the same amount, and the family summary shows that they agree.

An **owner and the business he owns** (a family and its business, a person and his own shop) agree entry by entry: money put in matches the business's capital, and *Income from {business}* matches the business's drawings. They do not agree in balance. The owner holds the business at the money he put in, while the business's own money also carries its profit, so the summary prints the difference as *profit kept in the business*. Where the business has paid out more than the owner's profit, the difference is negative and is printed as *taken out beyond the profit*.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §6, *Accounting for Independent Branches*, pp. 15.41–15.44, by analogy; owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (naming, and money put in staying at what was put in). The source teaches the pair for branches of one concern only, where the balances agree. Ruled O-16-1 (amended 20 Oct 2026).

**Example.** Running-account pair: the family gives Rajinder's household ₹10,000. Family's book: Dr Rajinder's household A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹10,000. Rajinder's book: Dr Cash A/c ₹10,000 · Cr Family A/c ₹10,000. Both say ₹10,000, on opposite sides.
Owner and business: the family puts ₹1,00,000 into Sharma Textile; the shop earns ₹80,000 and sends ₹20,000 home. Family's book: Dr Sharma Textile A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000; Dr Cash A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Income from Sharma Textile A/c ₹20,000. Shop's book: Dr Bank A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Family's own money A/c ₹1,00,000; Dr Drawings A/c ₹20,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹20,000. The entries match one for one. The family holds the shop at ₹1,00,000; the shop's own money is 1,00,000 + 80,000 − 20,000 = ₹1,60,000. The summary prints the ₹60,000 difference as *profit kept in the business*.
Diary: "The shop's ₹1,00,000 and the ₹20,000 sent home appear in both books. The shop has earned more than it sent home; ₹60,000 of profit is kept in the business, and the summary says so."

### 4.2 One person paying another's bill

When one party pays the other's bill from its own pocket, the payer's book debits the other's account and credits cash or bank, because the other owes it back. The other's book debits the expense and credits the payer's account. Between a family and a sub-family it goes through their running account. It is Own money only when an owner pays his own business's bill.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §6, table of entries (i)–(xiv), pp. 15.42–15.44, and §7.1, pp. 15.46–15.48 (head office pays the branch's expense: Dr Branch A/c, Cr Bank; the branch debits the expense and credits Head Office A/c), by analogy. Ruled O-16-2; the owner-pays-business case is O-12-5.

**Example.** The family pays ₹12,000 for the electricity of Rajinder's household.
Family's book: Dr Rajinder's household A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹12,000. Rajinder's book: Dr Electricity A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Family A/c ₹12,000.
Diary: family: "Paid ₹12,000 electricity for Rajinder's household." Rajinder: "Electricity ₹12,000, paid by the family."

### 4.3 Three parties need no go-between

When A pays B's bill and B owed C, no hub is needed. A's book: Dr *B A/c* · Cr cash or bank. B's book: Dr the expense (or Dr *C A/c* if B owed C) · Cr *A A/c*. Each book records only its own side.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §7.2(1), p. 15.48 (inter-branch items go through head office, "especially where the number of branches are large"); the books of a family and its members are peers, not branches, so no hub is used. Ruled O-16-3.

**Example.** Rajinder's household pays an electrician ₹2,500 for a bill owed by Harpreet's household.
Rajinder's book: Dr Harpreet's household A/c ₹2,500 · Cr Cash A/c ₹2,500. Harpreet's book: Dr Electricity A/c ₹2,500 · Cr Rajinder's household A/c ₹2,500.
Diary: Rajinder: "Paid the electrician ₹2,500 for Harpreet's household." Harpreet: "Electricity ₹2,500, paid for us by Rajinder; we now owe Rajinder, not the electrician."

### 4.4 When the two accounts disagree, correct the book that missed the item

For a running-account pair the two balances must agree. If they do not, the difference is cash or goods in transit, an item one side paid or collected directly and the other has not been told, or an item recorded at one end only. The fix is entered only in the book that did not record it; the book that recorded it in full makes no further entry. An owner and the business he owns do not agree in balance (4.1); they are checked entry by entry, and an entry found in only one book is entered in the other.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §7 and §7.1, worked example and "Important Points to be noted", pp. 15.44–15.48. The source reconciles a head office and its branch, where the balances agree; it is applied here to running-account pairs only.

**Example.** The family's Rajinder's household A/c reads ₹45,000 against the household; Rajinder's Family A/c reads ₹30,500 in the family's favour; the difference is ₹14,500. Rajinder's household paid ₹14,500 to a supplier of the family, and the family had not recorded it: in the family's book Dr Supplier A/c ₹14,500 · Cr Rajinder's household A/c ₹14,500. Both books now say ₹30,500.
Diary: "Our book and Rajinder's differed by ₹14,500. It was the ₹14,500 Rajinder's household had paid our supplier for us. We had not written it; now we have, in our book only. Both say ₹30,500: agrees."

### 4.5 Carried forward by default; settled when the two books agree

The pair carries its balance forward from year to year; nothing closes it. They settle it in money whenever both books agree to. A joint family's sub-family accounts follow their own rule above.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 15 §7.1 worked example, p. 15.47 (the reconciled balance carried forward as "To Balance b/d"). Settling in money is not covered by the source; ruled O-16-4.

**Example.** Rajinder's household account stands at ₹90,000 against him at the opening.
Family's book: Dr Rajinder's household A/c ₹90,000 (brought forward). The household pays ₹30,000 back: Dr Bank A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Rajinder's household A/c ₹30,000. Rajinder's book: Cr Family A/c ₹90,000 brought forward; Dr Family A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹30,000.
Diary: "Opening: Rajinder's household has taken ₹90,000 more than it put in. It paid in ₹30,000; ₹60,000 remains."

### 4.6 The statement the two books exchange

*Account between {Book A} and {Book B}* for a period shows both books' accounts side by side. Each has its opening balance, every entry (date, number, note) and its closing balance. A closing line says either *Agrees* or lists the entries found in only one book. It is the per-pair section of the family's summary.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-16-5): the branch chapter reconciles two ledger accounts but gives no statement form; related books need one.

**Example.**
Account between Family and Rajinder's household, 1 April–31 March: opening ₹90,000 against the household; money given ₹40,000; household put in ₹70,000; closing ₹60,000 against the household in the family's book and ₹60,000 in the family's favour in Rajinder's book; *Agrees*.
Diary: "At year end both books say ₹60,000. Agrees."

## 5. Trust, committee and not-for-profit

A trust, gurdwara or temple committee, society or club holds other people's gifts in trust for a purpose. Its book is kept in funds. It prints three statements from the same entries: a summary of cash received and paid, a year statement of income and expenditure, and the position at the date. The trust's own-money account is the General Fund.

### 5.1 Three statements from the same entries

Every trust or committee book prints Receipts & Payments, Income & Expenditure and the position (Balance Sheet) from the same entries, so a small committee that thinks only of its cash book loses nothing. Receipts & Payments is a summary of the cash book, with receipts of every period and of capital as well as revenue; it cannot show a surplus or deficit. Income & Expenditure holds the year's revenue items and no capital items, and its balance is the surplus or deficit. The Balance Sheet always accompanies it.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §1, p. 8.2, §2 and §2.1, pp. 8.3–8.4 (Receipts & Payments alone only for the simplest bodies with no assets but cash and no liabilities); §3, §3.2–3.3, §4, pp. 8.5–8.11; ICAI Technical Guide on Accounting for NPOs, 2023 ed. paras 18–24, 100–103 and 2009 ed. ¶26–27, ¶32 (accrual recommended for all). Ruled O-17-9.

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Membership Fees A/c ₹3,000.
Diary: "Received ₹3,000 membership fee from Kapoor." (It appears in Receipts & Payments as *Received*, and in Income & Expenditure as the year's income.)

### 5.2 General Fund and Corpus

The General Fund is the committee's own money: every receipt not held in a fund (5.4) or as Corpus, less what is spent, carried year to year. Corpus is only the founders' or promoters' gift, given to start the trust. A donor who is not a founder and asks that the money be kept and only its income used gives an endowment, a fund of its own (5.4). Everything else is the General Fund. The year's surplus or deficit goes to the General Fund.

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 106(a)(i) (corpus is the non-reducible fund contributed by founders or promoters), 106(a)(iii) (the General Fund is the unrestricted money other than designated funds and corpus), 106(b) and 112 (an endowment, whose principal is not spent, is a restricted fund) and 109 (the year's surplus or deficit goes to the General Fund). The BoS's Capital Fund is wider (Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §4, pp. 8.10–8.11); the Guide is the higher source and is followed (ruled O-17-6; corpus limited to the founders' gift, owner ruling 22 Oct 2026).

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹10,00,000 · Cr Corpus A/c ₹10,00,000.
Diary: "The founders gave ₹10,00,000 to start the trust. It is Corpus."

### 5.3 General donations and legacies are income

A donation or legacy with no restriction on its use is Income in the Income & Expenditure account (*Income*, and then the General Fund through the surplus). A donation towards one of the trust's running activities, such as a *Dairy Donation* for the cows' feed or a donation for langar rations, is Income too, under its own head; the test that separates it from a fund is in 5.4. A legacy goes to a fund for a purpose only if the will restricts its use.

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 106(a) ("All incomes (donations, legacies, investment income, fees, etc.) not subject to external restrictions are part of unrestricted funds"); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 "Other concepts" item 1(b), p. 8.13, Illustration 4 (general donations credited to Income & Expenditure) and MCQ 3, p. 8.40 (answer (b), Income and Expenditure Account, p. 8.45). The BoS also says elsewhere that a legacy or an unspecified donation is added to the Capital Fund (§4.1.1, p. 8.11; "Other concepts" item 3, p. 8.13); the Guide is the higher source and is followed (ruled O-17-4, O-17-5). Owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (a *Dairy Donation*-style receipt for a running activity is income).

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹55,000 · Cr Donations A/c ₹55,000; Dr Bank A/c ₹2,00,000 · Cr Legacy A/c ₹2,00,000.
Diary: "Received ₹55,000 in general donations. A legacy of ₹2,00,000 came under the late Kapoor's will. Both are the year's income."

### 5.4 Money given for a purpose is held in a fund of its own

When the donor gives money for something to be built, bought or kept (a hall, a named appeal, an endowment), it is credited to a fund named for the purpose, not to the year's income, and shown separately in the position. It is spent only on that purpose. A donation towards one of the trust's running activities (the dairy, the langar), used up in the year's work, is not a fund: it is income under its own head (5.3). A fund is a purpose set aside; it is not a bank account.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §4.1.1, p. 8.11 (donations raised for revenue expenditure are credited to Income & Expenditure; those for a specific purpose, with the donor's intention, go to a special fund) — the test followed here, with owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (the dairy donation is income) and owner ruling, 22 Oct 2026 (the test); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §3.1, pp. 8.5–8.6; ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. paras 106(b), 110 (restricted funds are credited to a separate fund account when received and shown separately). Para 110 would place every donor-restricted receipt in a fund, revenue or capital; the book follows the BoS test instead.

**Example.**
Dr Cash A/c ₹1,00,000 · Cr Community Hall Fund A/c ₹1,00,000.
Diary: "Received ₹1,00,000 from Sharma for the community hall."

### 5.5 Spending from a fund is charged to expenditure, with a matching transfer from the fund

When the trust spends for the purpose of a fund, the spending is charged to Income & Expenditure and the same amount is transferred from the fund to an income head, *Transferred from the {fund} A/c*, which the year close carries to Income & Expenditure. The two postings are entered as one action: "paid from the Hall Fund". Every rupee spent is visible in Income & Expenditure, and the surplus on that fund is nil.

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. paras 101, 107, 110 (2009 ed. ¶111, ¶114). ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §4.1.1, p. 8.11, and "Other concepts" item 5, Illustration 5, debits the fund directly and says the expense is on no account charged to Income & Expenditure; the two ICAI sources differ and the Technical Guide, the higher source, is followed (ruled O-17-1).

**Example.** ₹30,000 for hall repairs, from the Hall Fund.
Dr Hall Repairs A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹30,000; Dr Community Hall Fund A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Transferred from the Community Hall Fund A/c ₹30,000 (income; closed to Income & Expenditure at the year end).
Diary: "Paid ₹30,000 for hall repairs, out of the Hall Fund." (One action; two postings.)

### 5.6 Interest on a fund's investment goes as the donor said

Interest earned on money invested from a fund goes where the donor's terms say. If the terms say nothing, it goes to that fund, so a fund's fixed deposit grows that fund.

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 111 (depends on the terms); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §4.1.1, p. 8.11 (income on a fund's investments is credited to the fund). Ruled O-17-2.

**Example.** The Hall Fund's fixed deposit earns ₹6,000; the terms say nothing.
Dr Bank A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Community Hall Fund A/c ₹6,000. If the terms allow general use: Dr Bank A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Interest Income A/c ₹6,000.
Diary: "Interest of ₹6,000 on the hall's fixed deposit came in; added to the Hall Fund." Or: "…counted as ordinary income, because the donor allowed it."

### 5.7 A building or other thing bought from a fund is held at cost, and the fund moves to the General Fund

A thing the trust buys with a fund's money is carried at what it cost (a thing owned, not an expense). When it is bought, the fund is transferred to the General Fund up to the cost, because the fund's purpose is fulfilled. A trust that opts into depreciation (see *04 — Year end and reports*) instead releases the fund to Income & Expenditure year by year in step with the depreciation. A building under construction is held at cost as *Building under construction* and moves to the finished building when complete; a trust or committee may instead charge construction as an expense of the year, saying so in its statements (the standing note in *04 — Year end and reports*, 4.3).

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 110 (for a depreciable asset the fund is treated as deferred income; for a non-depreciable asset it is transferred to a *Capital Reserve*). The book departs from para 110 on the non-depreciable case and moves the fund to the General Fund instead, so the trust keeps one own-money account (owner ruling, 22 Oct 2026); para 109(a) likewise keeps grants for a non-depreciable asset within the General Fund. ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §3.1, p. 8.6 (expenditure on a fixed asset is capitalised, not an expense). With no depreciation by default the asset behaves as non-depreciable (ruled O-17-3). Construction as an expense of the year: owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (ruled O-09-7).

**Example.** A hall is built for ₹5,00,000 from the Hall Fund.
Dr Hall Building A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹5,00,000; then Dr Community Hall Fund A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr General Fund A/c ₹5,00,000.
Diary: "Paid ₹5,00,000 to build the hall. The Hall Fund's purpose is done; ₹5,00,000 moves to the General Fund." If the trust charges construction to the year, with ₹3,00,000 from the Hall Fund and ₹2,00,000 from general money: Dr Hall Construction A/c ₹5,00,000 · Cr Bank A/c ₹5,00,000; Dr Community Hall Fund A/c ₹3,00,000 · Cr Transferred from the Community Hall Fund A/c ₹3,00,000 (as in the rule on spending from a fund). The part met from the fund nets to nil; only the ₹2,00,000 paid from general money falls on the year, as a deficit, and the statements say so.

### 5.8 Things donated and kept are entered at a nominal rupee

A thing donated and kept (land, furniture, a water cooler) is recorded at ₹1, with a description and the estimated value in the note. Goods used up at once, such as langar rations, are not entered as money.

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. paras 49(b), 49(h) and 135 (an asset received free of cost is recorded at a nominal value; assets in kind not previously recorded come in at cost "which may be at nominal values"). ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §4.1.1 and §5.2.1 credit the asset's valuation to the fund; the Guide is the higher source (ruled O-17-7).

**Example.**
Dr Furniture A/c ₹1 · Cr Donations A/c ₹1 (income; note: "Steel cupboard, given by Sharma; about ₹40,000").
Diary: "Sharma gave the society a cupboard worth about ₹40,000. Entered at ₹1, with the description kept in the note."

### 5.9 A donation box (gollak, hundi) is recorded when it is counted

The count records the donation. Debit *Gollak* (the counted cash in the box) and credit *Donations – gollak* (or the fund, if the box is for something to be built, bought or kept, 5.4). Emptying the box into the cash box or bank is a transfer: debit cash or bank, credit *Gollak*. Money not yet counted is not recorded.

**Source:** none settles it — ruled 20 Oct 2026 (O-17-8): no accounting source read mentions a donation box (a news report on a large trust describes custody only: sealed boxes, two keys, counted by two people, entered after the bank's receipt). The rule follows general donations, ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 106(a).

**Example.**
Dr Gollak A/c ₹14,200 · Cr Donations – gollak A/c ₹14,200; then Dr Bank A/c ₹14,200 · Cr Gollak A/c ₹14,200.
Diary: "Opened the donation box in front of two committee members and counted ₹14,200. Next day the ₹14,200 went to the bank."

### 5.10 Subscriptions are recorded when received

Member subscriptions are recorded when received, by default. A committee that tracks dues keeps one account per member and charges the subscription when due, like rent in the landlord section: a due with a named person.

**Source:** ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 19 (the cash basis records income when received); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §4.1.3, pp. 8.11–8.12 (subscription account with outstanding and advance balances). A member-by-member khata is not described; ruled O-17-10 on the khata basis.

**Example.**
By default: Dr Cash A/c ₹500 · Cr Subscriptions A/c ₹500. Tracking dues: Dr Verma A/c ₹500 · Cr Subscriptions A/c ₹500 when due; Dr Cash A/c ₹500 · Cr Verma A/c ₹500 when paid.
Diary: "Verma paid his ₹500 subscription." Or: "Verma's ₹500 is due this month; he paid it on the 12th."

### 5.11 What is particular to the trust's year close

The book posts the closing entries itself: income and expenditure heads to Income & Expenditure; the surplus (or deficit) to the General Fund. A fund's balance carries forward in its own account. Corpus is untouched. See *04 — Year end and reports* for the three printed statements.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §3.3(iv), p. 8.7 (the balance of Income & Expenditure is transferred to the Accumulated or Capital Fund) and Illustration 4, pp. 8.15–8.16 (the ₹57,000 surplus of the example); ICAI Technical Guide on NPOs, 2023 ed. para 109 (surplus or deficit goes to the General Fund). Ruled O-19-1.

**Example.**
Dr Income & Expenditure A/c ₹57,000 · Cr General Fund A/c ₹57,000.
Diary: "The year's income was ₹57,000 more than the year's spending. The ₹57,000 is added to the General Fund."

## 6. Landlord

A landlord's book is the owner's own book (a household's or a business's, as in the first section). Its year statement follows the book the rent is kept in: a household that lets property keeps *Income & Expenses* with the result *Saving* (1.10), rent being one of its income heads, and stays on the khata basis; a landlord's business book keeps *Profit & Loss*. What is particular to it is the account for each tenant, and the way rent, advance rent, deposits and property spending are kept.

### 6.1 Rent is charged to the tenant's account when it falls due

Rent is a due with a named person, so it is recorded when due, not only when received: it is debited to the tenant's account and credited to Rent. When the tenant pays, the receipt is credited to his account. One entry may charge several tenants.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 3 (a customer on credit has a personal account), by analogy; NCERT Class 11 Part I, ch. 3, the journal (compound entries, pp. 62–63). No source is written for a landlord. Ruled O-18-1 on the khata basis: a due with a named person is recorded when it arises.

**Example.** Rent for April: Sharma ₹6,000, Verma ₹7,000.
Dr Sharma (Tenant) A/c ₹6,000 · Dr Verma (Tenant) A/c ₹7,000 · Cr Rent A/c ₹13,000. When Sharma pays: Dr Cash A/c ₹6,000 · Cr Sharma (Tenant) A/c ₹6,000.
Diary: "April rent: Sharma ₹6,000, Verma ₹7,000, total ₹13,000. Sharma paid on the 5th. You will get ₹7,000 from Verma."

### 6.2 A tenant who has paid ahead is in credit on his own account

When a tenant pays rent ahead, the receipt is credited to his own account, which then stands in his favour and shows under *You will give*. Each month's rent charged reduces it. There is no separate "rent received in advance" account on the landlord's page.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part II, ch. 9 §9.6 (rent received in advance is a liability, not income of the year); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 2 Unit 1 §1.8 table, p. 2.29(r) ("rent received in advance" is a personal account and a liability). The sources show one general account; the per-person form is that same liability by person (ruled O-18-2, on one account per person).

**Example.** Verma pays ₹9,000 on 20 March for April–June; April's rent of ₹3,000 is then charged.
Dr Cash A/c ₹9,000 · Cr Verma (Tenant) A/c ₹9,000. April: Dr Verma (Tenant) A/c ₹3,000 · Cr Rent A/c ₹3,000.
Diary: "Verma paid ₹9,000 three months ahead. After April's ₹3,000 is charged, he is ₹6,000 ahead with us: You will give ₹6,000."

### 6.3 A tenant's security deposit has an account of its own

A security deposit is held in its own account for each tenant, *Deposit held – {tenant}*, a liability while held. It is never netted into the tenant's running account for rent. It leaves in one of three ways: returned (cash or bank); set off against rent or dues when agreed, with no money moving; or forfeited to income as *Deposit forfeited*.

**Source:** owner ruling, 13 Oct 2026 (a deposit has its own account per person). ICAI Framework for the Preparation and Presentation of Financial Statements, ¶49 (a liability); ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 8 §5.2.3, p. 8.31 and Illustration 12 (a deposit held from students shown as a liability). No source treats a landlord's deposit; return, set-off and forfeiture are ruled (O-09-8, O-18-3).

**Example.**
Received: Dr Cash A/c ₹50,000 · Cr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹50,000.
Set off against unpaid rent of ₹12,000, by agreement: Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹12,000 · Cr Sharma (Tenant) A/c ₹12,000.
Returned: Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹38,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹38,000.
Or, if ₹5,000 of it is forfeited by agreement: returned Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹33,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹33,000; forfeited Dr Deposit held – Sharma A/c ₹5,000 · Cr Deposit forfeited A/c ₹5,000.
Diary: "Sharma gave ₹50,000 deposit. ₹12,000 was set against rent he owed. The remaining ₹38,000 was returned when he left." Or: "…₹33,000 was returned and ₹5,000 kept, as agreed." (A forfeited amount is income, *Deposit forfeited*.)

### 6.4 A repair is an expense; an improvement is added to the building's cost

Day-to-day servicing and repair is an expense when paid. Spending that raises what the building yields (an extra room, a replaced major part) is added to the building's cost as a thing owned; when a major part is replaced, the cost of the part taken out is removed at the same time (*02*, 5.3). Replacing a worn-out part is a repair. No depreciation is charged by default.

**Source:** ICAI BoS Foundation Paper 1, Ch. 1 Unit 3 §3.2(c)–(e), p. 1.63 (an improvement that increases expected benefits is added to the asset; repairs are expense; materiality counts); ICAI BoS Intermediate Paper 1, Ch. 5 Unit 2 §2.10, pp. 5.38–5.40 (AS 10 study material on subsequent costs). Ruled O-18-4; no depreciation by default (see *04 — Year end and reports*).

**Example.**
Whitewash: Dr Repairs and Maintenance A/c ₹8,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹8,000. Extra room: Dr House (Building) A/c ₹2,00,000 · Cr Cash A/c ₹2,00,000.
Diary: "Paid ₹8,000 for whitewash: an expense. Paid ₹2,00,000 to add a room: added to what the house cost."

### 6.5 Rent a tenant will not pay is written off

Rent that will not come back is written off to *Rent written off*, an expense head, and credited to the tenant's account.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part II, ch. 9 §9.8 (bad debts), extended; owner ruling, 12 Oct 2026 (a *Written off* head). Ruled O-12-1, O-18-5.

**Example.**
Dr Rent written off A/c ₹7,000 · Cr Verma (Tenant) A/c ₹7,000.
Diary: "Verma left owing ₹7,000 and will not pay. Written off as an expense."

### 6.6 What is particular to the landlord's year close

The book posts the closing entries itself: income (*Rent*, *Deposit forfeited*) and expense heads to the result; the result to the owner's Own money — in a business book the profit, with any drawings; in a household the saving (1.10). A tenant's balance and a deposit held carry forward in their own accounts. See *04 — Year end and reports*.

**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Part II, ch. 8 §8.6.2 items (7)–(8); ruled O-19-1; a household's statement, ruled O-13-5 (1.10).

**Example.** A landlord's business book:
Dr Rent A/c ₹1,56,000 · Cr Profit & Loss A/c ₹1,56,000; Dr Profit & Loss A/c ₹30,000 · Cr Repairs and Maintenance A/c ₹30,000; Dr Profit & Loss A/c ₹1,26,000 · Cr Own money A/c ₹1,26,000.
Diary: "Rent for the year ₹1,56,000, repairs ₹30,000. Profit ₹1,26,000, added to my own money."
